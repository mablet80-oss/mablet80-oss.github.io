20:T1d9f,<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>In South Korea, "botox" is the everyday word for any botulinum toxin injection, the way "hoover" can mean any vacuum cleaner. Botox itself is Allergan's brand, now sold by AbbVie.</p>
<p>Most Korean clinics list several toxins side by side: Korean brands such as <strong>Botulax</strong>, <strong>Nabota</strong> and <strong>Meditoxin</strong>, and imported brands such as <strong>Botox</strong> and <strong>Xeomin</strong>. The Korean brands are usually the default and much cheaper.</p>
<p>All of them are licensed as drugs by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS). The choice is about brand, price and preference, not about legal versus illegal product.</p>

<p>The MFDS lists around 60 botulinum toxin products in total, several of them for export only. Any product a clinic names can be looked up on the ministry's <a href="https://nedrug.mfds.go.kr/searchDrug?itemName=%EB%B3%B4%ED%88%B4%EB%A6%AC%EB%88%94">의약품안전나라</a> site, which shows its licence and approved uses. The Korea Pharmaceutical Information Center (약학정보원, health.kr) shows photos of each product's box and vial, useful for checking what you are given.</p>
<p>Clinics also use toxins for uses not on these licences, such as the jaw for most brands. That is common practice, but it is worth knowing which use the product was actually tested for.</p>
<h2 id="two-korean-toxins-are-fda-approved">Two Korean toxins are FDA approved</h2>
<p>Two Korean products have passed the US Food and Drug Administration's review for frown lines:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nabota</strong> (Daewoong) is sold in the US as <strong>Jeuveau</strong>, first approved in 2019. The same toxin is sold as Nuceiva in Australia.</li>
<li><strong>Botulax</strong> (Hugel) was approved as <strong>Letybo</strong> on 29 February 2024. It is also approved in Australia.</li>
</ul>
<p>So a Korean brand is not an unknown quantity. If you are used to Jeuveau or Letybo at home, the Korean versions are Nabota and Botulax.</p>
<h2 id="how-they-compare-in-trials">How they compare in trials</h2>
<p>Several Korean toxins have been tested directly against Botox in randomised, double-blind trials for frown lines, usually at the same labelled dose of 20 units:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nabota</strong> against Botox in 268 subjects: 93.89% improved at week 4 against 88.64%, non-inferior.</li>
<li><strong>Nabota</strong> (as prabotulinumtoxinA) against Botox and placebo in 540 subjects in Europe and Canada: 87.2% responded against 82.8%, non-inferior.</li>
<li><strong>Botulax</strong> against Botox in 500 subjects in China: 88.49% responded against 87.39%, non-inferior.</li>
<li>A <strong>Huons</strong> toxin against Botox in 267 subjects: 90.15% against 92.31%, non-inferior.</li>
</ul>
<p>Non-inferior means the product was not meaningfully worse than Botox in that trial. It does not mean the two are identical, and most of these trials were funded by the manufacturer and run in Korean patients.</p>
<p>For an everyday frown-line treatment, the evidence supports choosing a licensed Korean brand on price and preference.</p>
<h2 id="units-are-not-interchangeable">Units are not interchangeable</h2>
<p>This is the most useful thing to know when comparing quotes. The FDA labels for Botox, Jeuveau and Letybo each say the same thing: units of one product <strong>cannot be compared with or converted into</strong> units of another. The Korean Xeomin licence says another toxin's dose cannot be substituted for its own.</p>
<p>So "50 units" from two different brands is not the same dose. Compare prices within one brand, and ask the clinic which product and how many units of it they plan to use.</p>
<h2 id="price-the-brand-sets-it">Price: the brand sets it</h2>
<p>The brand is the biggest single factor in a Korean toxin price. In November 2026, Seoul clinics listed 50 units of jaw botox at:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>₩18,600 to ₩53,900</strong> with a Korean brand (13 clinics, median ₩38,500)</li>
<li><strong>₩86,900 to ₩148,500</strong> with Xeomin or Allergan (11 clinics, median ₩119,900)</li>
</ul>
<p>The same clinic usually lists both. Some of the lowest Korean-brand prices were for split vials, and some clinics charge more to open a new vial in front of you. The <a href="/cost/seoul-skin-treatment-prices/">Seoul price overview</a> has the method and the full dataset.</p>
<h2 id="pure-toxins-and-resistance">"Pure" toxins and resistance</h2>
<p>Xeomin and Coretox are sold as "pure" toxins: the core toxin without the complexing proteins the others carry. The idea is that fewer proteins mean less chance of developing antibodies that stop the toxin working.</p>
<p>The evidence so far is reassuring for everyone. An overview of five systematic reviews covering 17,815 patients found neutralising antibodies rare in aesthetic use, at 1 to 2.1% across the toxins studied, with no significant difference between them. A clinical advantage for "pure" toxins is plausible but has not been shown in an adequately powered trial.</p>
<p>If a toxin has stopped working for you before, tell the clinic. Switching brands is a reasonable conversation to have.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-the-clinic">What to ask the clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which product exactly</strong>, by brand name, in Korean if possible.</li>
<li><strong>How many units</strong> of that product, for which area.</li>
<li><strong>Whether the vial is opened for you</strong>, and whether that costs extra.</li>
<li><strong>Whether the area is on that product's licence</strong>, if that matters to you.</li>
</ul>

<p>In Korea, "botox" usually means a Korean-made botulinum toxin, licensed by the MFDS, with Allergan's Botox and Merz's Xeomin offered at a higher price. Two Korean brands, Nabota and Botulax, are also FDA approved, and randomised trials found the Korean toxins tested non-inferior to Botox for frown lines.
