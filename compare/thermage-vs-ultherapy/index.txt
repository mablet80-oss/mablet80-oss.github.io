22:T1581,<h2 id="the-short-version">The short version</h2>
<p>Both heat tissue so that collagen contracts and more is laid down over the following months. They
differ in <strong>what they heat and how</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Thermage</strong> is monopolar radiofrequency. Current passes through tissue, and the tissue's own
resistance produces heat across a large volume, reaching up to around 20 mm.</p>
<p><strong>Ultherapy</strong> is microfocused ultrasound. Energy is focused to a point at a chosen depth, creating
small discrete zones of thermal injury — the deepest setting reaching the SMAS, the fibrous layer a
surgeon pulls on in a facelift.</p>
<p>Diffuse volume against precise points. That difference drives everything below.</p>

<p>The visualization is a genuine difference rather than marketing: the ultrasound device images the
layers before it fires, so the operator is placing energy at a depth they can see rather than
inferred.</p>
<h2 id="what-each-is-for">What each is for</h2>
<p>Neither is a lifting treatment in the sense most people mean. Both <strong>tighten tissue that is still in
place</strong>. Neither moves tissue that has descended, and neither adds volume.</p>
<p>Within that:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Monopolar radiofrequency</strong> heats broadly, so it is generally described for overall laxity and
contour across a region.</li>
<li><strong>Focused ultrasound</strong> places energy at chosen depths, including the SMAS, which is the basis for
its use on the lower face and neck.</li>
</ul>
<p>That is as far as the evidence supports a distinction. What it does not support is a confident claim
that one outperforms the other for a given face, and no head-to-head trial settles it.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-numbers-actually-say">What the numbers actually say</h2>
<p>For <strong>focused ultrasound</strong>, a 2024 meta-analysis of 42 studies: investigators rated <strong>89%</strong> of
patients improved (95% CI 81–94%), patients rated themselves <strong>84%</strong> (95% CI 73–91%), mean pain
<strong>4.85 of 10</strong>, no serious adverse events reported.</p>
<p>For <strong>monopolar radiofrequency</strong>, the 2025 review reports a multicentre trial with <strong>83.2%</strong>
periorbital improvement and eyebrow lifting over 0.5 mm in <strong>61.5%</strong> of 86 patients, with
satisfaction across studies of <strong>50% to 82%</strong>.</p>
<p>Two cautions worth more than the numbers themselves.</p>
<p><strong>The radiofrequency satisfaction range is enormous.</strong> Half to four-fifths is not a rounding
difference. The reviewers attribute the spread to treatment parameters and operator expertise, not
to the technology.</p>
<p><strong>The ultrasound satisfaction figure moves with the question.</strong> Pooled satisfaction was 84% — and
<strong>62%</strong> in the studies that offered a neutral answer instead of forcing a positive or negative. When
a clinic quotes a satisfaction percentage, ask how it was asked.</p>
<h2 id="pain-and-what-it-buys">Pain, and what it buys</h2>
<p>Depth costs comfort. Monopolar radiofrequency is the most uncomfortable of the radiofrequency
arrangements because it is heating a large volume, and it needs surface cooling to protect the skin
— with the trade-off that more cooling works against the depth being paid for.</p>
<p>Focused ultrasound is felt most at the deepest settings, and pooled at 4.85 of 10 it sits in the
moderate range.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-actually-decide">How to actually decide</h2>
<p>Not by technology. By these:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>What are you trying to change</strong> — general laxity across a region, or a specific area like the
jawline and upper neck?</li>
<li><strong>What is the operator's experience with the device in front of you?</strong> Both bodies of evidence
land on operator expertise as the variable that moves outcomes.</li>
<li><strong>What is being claimed?</strong> A specific promise about your result is not something either evidence
base supports.</li>
<li><strong>Is the device licensed here?</strong> Ask for its Korean product name and check it.</li>
</ul>
<p>If the honest answer to the first question is "tissue has descended", then the answer is neither
device, and a page about threads or a conversation about surgery is the right next step.</p>
