1d:T1281,<h2 id="two-problems-that-look-like-one">Two problems that look like one</h2>
<p>Almost every disappointment in this category comes from treating the wrong one.</p>
<p><strong>Laxity</strong> is tissue that is still where it belongs but has lost firmness. <strong>Descent</strong> is tissue that
has moved down — a jowl, a heavy fold, a lower face that has changed shape.</p>
<p>Most treatments marketed as "lifting" address the first. Very few address the second, and the ones
that do are more invasive. Working out which you have, and being told honestly, is most of the
decision.</p>
<h2 id="what-tightens">What tightens</h2>
<p><strong>Focused ultrasound</strong> and <strong>radiofrequency</strong> heat tissue so collagen contracts and more is laid
down over the following months. They differ in depth and delivery — ultrasound at fixed depths
including the SMAS, radiofrequency diffusely through a volume — but neither moves anything.</p>
<p>The pooled evidence for focused ultrasound is the strongest in this category: a 2024 meta-analysis
of <strong>42 studies</strong> found <strong>89%</strong> of patients rated improved by investigators (95% CI 81–94%) and
<strong>84%</strong> by themselves (95% CI 73–91%), with a generally favourable safety profile.</p>
<p>Read that as most people seeing some change, gradually. Not as a facelift.</p>
<p>One caution on the satisfaction numbers that applies across this whole category: the same analysis
found pooled satisfaction of 84% falling to <strong>62%</strong> in studies that let patients answer neutrally.
Published satisfaction for monopolar radiofrequency ranges from <strong>50% to 82%</strong>. Any single figure
quoted at you is a selection from a wide field.</p>
<h2 id="what-repositions">What repositions</h2>
<p>A <strong>thread lift</strong> passes barbed absorbable sutures under the skin and draws tissue upward. It is
mechanical, and you see it on the day.</p>
<p>The published follow-up is specific, and it is the number to hold a clinic to: one series maintained
<strong>70%</strong> of the initial correction at <strong>12–24 months</strong>, another <strong>60%</strong> at <strong>36 months</strong>. So it
fades, and a meaningful part of what was paid for is gone within a few years.</p>
<p>Complications are common though mostly minor — a 2026 meta-analysis of 26 studies and 2,827
patients found swelling in 34%, bruising 26%, and <strong>visible or palpable threads in 10%</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="when-surgery-is-the-honest-answer">When surgery is the honest answer</h2>
<p>The 2017 review of thread lifting reaches a conclusion that is unusually direct for this field and
worth quoting rather than paraphrasing: thread lifting <em>"should not yet be adopted as an alternative
to rhytidectomy"</em>, with results described as subtle and of short duration compared with surgery.</p>
<p>That does not make thread lifting pointless. It means there is an amount of descent past which
suspension on absorbable sutures will not hold, and past that point the non-surgical options are
managing an expectation rather than the problem.</p>
<p>A clinic willing to tell you that is more valuable than one that will sell you three courses first.</p>
<h2 id="what-none-of-them-do">What none of them do</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Restore volume.</strong> A hollow is a filler or collagen stimulator question.</li>
<li><strong>Improve skin quality.</strong> Texture and pigment belong to resurfacing and boosters.</li>
<li><strong>Work on the day</strong>, in the case of the energy devices. Change accrues over months, and judging at
two weeks is judging too early.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-at-a-consultation">What to ask at a consultation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Is my problem laxity or descent?</strong> Make them answer this first.</li>
<li>If an energy device is proposed for descended tissue, what is the reasoning?</li>
<li>Which device or thread, and what is its Korean product name?</li>
<li>What specifically do you expect to change, and when should I judge it?</li>
<li>If both a device and threads are proposed, what is each one for?</li>
<li>At what point would you tell me this is a surgical question?</li>
</ul>

to two years and 60% at three. A review of the literature concluded thread lifting is not yet an
