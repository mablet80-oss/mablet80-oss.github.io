1e:T183d,<h2 id="which-lines-are-you-actually-looking-at">Which lines are you actually looking at</h2>
<p>Stand in front of a mirror and stop moving your face.</p>
<p>The lines that <strong>disappear</strong> are <strong>dynamic</strong> — made by muscle. The lines still there are <strong>static</strong> —
the skin itself has changed. And some of what people call wrinkles is neither: a <strong>fold</strong> produced
by lost volume or descended tissue, which no wrinkle treatment touches.</p>
<p>Three different mechanisms. Three different answers. Getting this wrong is the reason people report
that a treatment "didn't work" when in fact it was never aimed at their problem.</p>
<h2 id="movement-lines-the-strongest-evidence-on-this-site">Movement lines: the strongest evidence on this site</h2>
<p>Botulinum toxin relaxes the muscle that folds the skin, and the effect sizes are larger than
anything else covered here.</p>
<p>A <strong>GRADE-assessed meta-analysis</strong> of three Phase III randomised trials and <strong>704 participants</strong>
found, at day 30 against placebo, relative risk of at least one grade of improvement of:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>21.49</strong> for glabellar frown lines (95% CI 9.89–46.73)</li>
<li><strong>19.17</strong> for horizontal forehead lines (9.23–39.80)</li>
<li><strong>11.68</strong> for lateral canthal lines (7.49–18.20)</li>
</ul>
<p>All at p &#x3C; 0.00001, with a Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale difference of 2.29 points. Serious
adverse events were not more frequent than on placebo.</p>
<p>For context: most treatments discussed on this site produce modest differences in a wide confidence
interval. This one produces a twenty-fold difference in the odds of visible improvement. When
someone says "the science on aesthetics is thin", this is the exception.</p>
<p>Two honest notes. The evidence is for <strong>upper facial lines</strong>, which is where the trials were run —
not for every use a clinic will suggest. And the ≥2-grade figures, though enormous, carry confidence
intervals wide enough to read as "far better than placebo" rather than as a number.</p>
<h2 id="static-lines-the-cheap-option-with-real-trials">Static lines: the cheap option with real trials</h2>
<p>A line that stays at rest needs the skin to change, and the best-evidenced way to do that is a
prescription cream.</p>
<p>A meta-analysis of <strong>eight randomised trials and 1,361 patients</strong> found <strong>topical tretinoin</strong>
significantly improved:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fine wrinkles</strong> — MD <strong>0.412</strong> (95% CI 0.233–0.590)</li>
<li><strong>Coarse wrinkles</strong> — MD <strong>0.245</strong> (95% CI 0.119–0.370)</li>
</ul>
<p>Read those honestly: they are <strong>modest</strong>. Tretinoin is not going to do what an image on a clinic
wall implies. But the effect is real, randomised, sustained over follow-ups from 16 weeks to two
years, and costs a fraction of any procedure.</p>
<p>The resurfacing treatments covered elsewhere on this site — fractional laser, microneedle
radiofrequency — work on the same problem by a different route, and belong in the conversation. What
should not happen is a procedure course proposed with no topical plan attached.</p>
<h2 id="what-tightening-devices-do-and-do-not">What tightening devices do, and do not</h2>
<p>Focused ultrasound and radiofrequency <strong>tighten tissue that has become slack</strong>. The pooled evidence
for focused ultrasound is strong on that measure — 89% investigator-rated improvement across 42
studies.</p>
<p>They do not erase a crease made by repeated movement, and they do not replace lost volume. If a
device is proposed for one specific line, ask what mechanism is supposed to remove it. The answer
should be more specific than "it tightens".</p>
<h2 id="when-it-is-a-fold-not-a-wrinkle">When it is a fold, not a wrinkle</h2>
<p>A nasolabial fold, a marionette line, a hollow under the eye — these are frequently sold as wrinkle
treatments and frequently are not.</p>
<p>If the cause is <strong>volume loss</strong>, that is a filler or collagen-stimulator question. If the cause is
<strong>descent</strong>, it belongs with sagging. Toxin and resurfacing will not resolve either, and a clinic
proposing them for a fold is either misreading the face or selling what it has.</p>
<h2 id="the-one-thing-that-prevents-them">The one thing that prevents them</h2>
<p>Nothing else in this field has evidence like this, and it costs nothing at a clinic.</p>
<p>Over <strong>4.5 years</strong>, <strong>903 adults</strong> were randomised to daily or discretionary sunscreen use, with
photoaging graded by blinded assessors. The <strong>daily sunscreen group showed no detectable increase in
skin aging</strong>, and <strong>24% less</strong> aging than the discretionary group (relative odds 0.76; 95% CI
0.59–0.98).</p>
<p>The same trial found <strong>β-carotene supplementation had no overall effect</strong> — which is worth
remembering the next time an oral "skin" supplement is added to a treatment package.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-at-a-consultation">What to ask at a consultation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Is this line dynamic, static, or a fold?</strong> Get the answer before the plan.</li>
<li>If toxin — <strong>which product</strong>, in Korean, and how many units where?</li>
<li>If resurfacing — <strong>what topical am I on afterwards</strong>, and for how long?</li>
<li>If a device is proposed for a specific line, <strong>what is the mechanism</strong>?</li>
<li>What should I expect at four weeks, and when should I judge it?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Separate the lines that move from the lines that stay, and both from folds caused by volume loss.
Botulinum toxin has the largest effect size on this site for movement lines — around twenty times
the odds of improvement against placebo across 704 patients. Static photodamage lines respond
modestly but reliably to topical tretinoin across 1,361 patients. Tightening devices address laxity,
not creases. And daily sunscreen is the only intervention here shown across 4.5 years to stop the
