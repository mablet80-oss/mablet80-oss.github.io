22:T19c3,<h2 id="four-problems-one-word">Four problems, one word</h2>
<p>Almost every wasted course in this category comes from treating the wrong one.</p>
<p><strong>Melasma.</strong> Patterned, usually symmetrical facial pigment, driven by ultraviolet light and
hormones. Chronic. It recurs.</p>
<p><strong>Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.</strong> The brown mark left behind by a spot, a burn, or a
procedure. It fades on its own, given time and sun protection.</p>
<p><strong>Sun spots and freckles.</strong> Discrete, in sun-exposed places, from accumulated ultraviolet damage.
The most straightforwardly treatable of the four.</p>
<p><strong>Dermal pigment.</strong> Deeper pigment, including the bilateral patches often confused with melasma. It
sits at a different depth and answers to different settings.</p>
<p>These look alike in a mirror and behave completely differently under treatment. So the first
question at a consultation is not <em>which laser</em> — it is <strong>which of these do I have, and in what
proportion?</strong> A clinic that quotes a package before answering that has not assessed you.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-has-the-best-evidence-for-melasma">What actually has the best evidence for melasma</h2>
<p>Not a device. This is the finding most clinic menus have no reason to tell you.</p>
<p>A <strong>network meta-analysis</strong> compared routes of <strong>tranexamic acid</strong> for melasma. <strong>Oral tranexamic
acid combined with routine topical agents</strong> produced a greater reduction in melasma severity than
intradermal injection, topical application, or microneedled delivery — at weeks 4, 8 and 12 and at
last follow-up. Oral also acted <strong>faster</strong> than every other route.</p>
<p>A separate meta-analysis of <strong>22 studies and 1,280 patients</strong> found the same ordering: oral first,
then injection, then topical.</p>
<p>Two things follow.</p>
<p><strong>First</strong>, the most-evidenced melasma treatment is a tablet plus a cream, which no clinic profits
from selling you a course of. That does not make it right for you — it is a prescription medicine
with contraindications, and the reviews stress screening and cautious patient selection. It makes it
the thing to ask about.</p>
<p><strong>Second</strong>, if a clinic proposes an expensive device course and never mentions systemic or topical
treatment, the plan is incomplete regardless of the device.</p>
<h2 id="where-lasers-do-and-do-not-fit">Where lasers do and do not fit</h2>
<p>For <strong>melasma</strong>, the pooled evidence is specific and counterintuitive. Across <strong>52 randomised
trials</strong> and 1,058 participants:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG</strong> — the classic "laser toning" — significantly beat controls (MD
<strong>1.47</strong>; 95% CI 0.08–2.85).</li>
<li><strong>Picosecond lasers</strong> — newer and more expensive — <strong>did not</strong> (MD −0.11; 95% CI −0.79 to 0.57).</li>
<li>The overall pooled analysis was a <strong>non-significant trend</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<p>The reviewers describe lasers here as <strong>adjunctive tools</strong>, and say directly that they should not be
considered a panacea.</p>
<p>For <strong>sun spots</strong>, pigment-targeting lasers are on much firmer ground — a discrete lesion with a
defined target is the case these devices were built for. The melasma finding is not an argument
against lasers in general. It is an argument against buying one for melasma on the assumption that
newer is better.</p>
<p>And laser carries a specific risk in this category: the same review reports post-inflammatory
hyper- <strong>and</strong> hypopigmentation, particularly at high fluences. In pigmented skin, the treatment can
create the problem.</p>
<h2 id="peels-and-what-they-tell-you-about-force">Peels, and what they tell you about force</h2>
<p>Peels work as single agents for melasma in darker skin. In a meta-analysis of <strong>478 patients</strong>,
<strong>glycolic acid outperformed trichloroacetic acid</strong> (MD −1.89; 95% CI −3.26 to −0.52).</p>
<p>Notice the pattern across this page. Twice, the gentler and cheaper option won: <strong>toning over
picosecond</strong>, <strong>glycolic acid over trichloroacetic acid</strong>. Melasma responds poorly to force, and
aggressive treatment in pigmented skin risks making it worse.</p>
<h2 id="the-part-that-decides-your-result">The part that decides your result</h2>
<p>Every source here converges on the same unglamorous point.</p>
<p>The peel meta-analysis concludes peels are effective <em>"with the use of topicals as maintenance
treatment"</em>. A sequential peel protocol saw <strong>70% recurrence within twelve weeks</strong> of stopping. The
tranexamic acid literature reports recurrence once treatment ends.</p>
<p>And the only intervention in this whole field with a long randomised trial behind it is the free
one: in a <strong>4.5-year trial of 903 adults</strong>, the <strong>daily sunscreen</strong> group showed <strong>no detectable
increase in skin aging</strong>, with <strong>24% less</strong> aging than the discretionary-use group.</p>
<p>If you are flying in for a course of treatment, the course is the beginning. What you do for the
following year is the treatment.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-at-a-consultation">What to ask at a consultation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which type of pigmentation do I have?</strong> Make this the first answer you get.</li>
<li>If melasma — <strong>what is the topical and systemic plan</strong>, not just the device plan?</li>
<li>Which device, at what fluence, and <strong>why that for my skin type</strong>?</li>
<li><strong>What is the plan if I darken</strong> after a session, and can it be handled before I fly home?</li>
<li><strong>What maintenance am I on afterwards</strong>, and can I get it at home?</li>
<li>What is the total cost of the course, not the session?</li>
</ul>

lasers did not. Glycolic acid beat trichloroacetic acid among peels. Everything recurs without
