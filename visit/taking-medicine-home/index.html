Taking medicine and skincare home from South Korea

Leaving Korea is rarely the problem. The rule that decides is the one where you land, and Japan caps cosmetics at 24 pieces per item.

Short answer

What can I take home from South Korea, and what needs permission?

The rule that decides is almost never Korea's, it is the one at the airport where you land, and it is set by your destination's health authority rather than by the clinic that prescribed anything. Japan is the strictest case for this audience and also the clearest: one month of prescription medicine, two months of ordinary medicine, 24 pieces of anything for external use, and 24 pieces per item of cosmetics, with an import certificate needed in advance above those figures. Controlled substances sit on a separate track everywhere and need paperwork arranged weeks ahead.

Key facts

Which rule governs

The rule that actually governs

People ask this question the wrong way round. They ask what Korea will let them take out, when the authority that decides is the one at the other end of the flight.

Korea is not generally where an ordinary personal quantity of medicine or skincare runs into trouble. Your own country's health regulator is, because importing medicine is a regulated act in most places and the traveller doing it is still an importer, just a small one.

So the practical question is not "can I take this out of Seoul". It is "how much of this may I bring into the place I live", and that answer changes by passport.

Japan, worked through in full

Japan is worth doing properly, both because it is the largest source of visitors to Korean clinics after China and because its health ministry publishes exact figures rather than a principle.

Without any import certificate, for personal use:

  • Prescription drugs. Up to one month's supply.
  • Drugs and quasi-drugs. Up to two months' supply.
  • Vitamins. Up to four months' supply.
  • External use items. Up to 24 pieces.
  • Cosmetics. Up to 24 pieces per item.
  • Medical devices. One set, for home use.

Two of those deserve a second read.

Cosmetics at 24 pieces per item is the line that catches people, because a Korean skincare haul is a normal part of this trip. Note the wording: the cap is per item, not per suitcase. Twelve different products in pairs is an easy pass. Twenty-five of the same sheet mask is not.

External use at 24 pieces is where a clinic's medicated creams and ointments land, which matters after any procedure that sends you home with topicals.

Above these figures you need an import certificate, applied for before you travel. It is a document with a lead time, not a form you fill in at the airport, so it only helps people who thought about it in advance.

Everywhere else

Other destinations use the same shape with different numbers, so the reliable move is to read your own regulator rather than a travel article.

For Australia, the Therapeutic Goods Administration publishes the traveller's exemption covering quantity, documentation and packaging. For most other countries the equivalent body is the national medicines or therapeutic goods regulator, and it is usually a single page on their site.

Whatever the figure turns out to be, three habits travel well. Keep things in original packaging with the dispensing label attached. Carry the prescription or a letter. Do not carry medicine for anybody else.

Controlled substances, both directions

This is a different category with a different process, and it rarely touches a skin clinic visit.

Narcotic and psychotropic medicines, which typically means opioids, benzodiazepines and stimulant medicines for attention disorders, are controlled almost everywhere and move only with paperwork. Korea requires ministry permission to bring such medicines into the country for personal treatment, and that application is made weeks ahead rather than on the day of the flight.

The reason to mention it on a page about going home is that the person affected is usually carrying their own regular medication into Korea, not leaving with something new.

What to ask the clinic for

Everything above works better with documents, and documents are easiest to get while you are still standing in the clinic.

Ask for the prescription itself, the product names written down rather than described, and where you can, an English-language certificate. That last one is a defined item on Korea's national fee schedule with its own ceiling rather than a favour you are requesting, and our page on your medical records and what they cost covers how that works.

A border officer reading a document reaches a decision quickly. A border officer looking at unlabelled boxes reaches a slower one.

The bottom line

Leaving Korea is seldom the constraint. Arriving home can be, and the numbers that bind are published by your own regulator.

For Japan the figures are specific enough to plan against, and the one to remember is 24 pieces per item of cosmetics, because that is the limit a good shopping day in Seoul actually reaches.

Frequently asked questions

How much Korean skincare can I bring into Japan?
Up to 24 pieces per item, and the phrase to notice is "per item" rather than in total. So a range of different products is treated more generously than twenty-five of the same serum. External-use medicines, which is where medicated creams and ointments from a clinic land, are capped at 24 pieces as well. These are the Japanese health ministry's published personal-use quantities, and going above them means an import certificate obtained before you travel.
Do I need a certificate for a prescription from a Korean clinic?
For Japan, not if it is a month's supply or less, which covers the usual course after a skin treatment. Other destinations set their own figure, and Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration publishes the exemption that applies there. The pattern is the same everywhere: an ordinary personal quantity, in its original packaging, with something in writing explaining what it is.
What counts as a controlled substance?
Narcotics and psychotropics as each country defines them, which typically catches opioids, benzodiazepines, and stimulant medicines for attention disorders. These almost never come out of a skin clinic, so this is usually a question about medicine you arrived with rather than medicine you are leaving with. If it applies to you, note that Korea requires ministry permission to bring such medicines in, and the application is made weeks before travel rather than on the day.
What should I ask the clinic for?
The prescription itself, the product names written down, and where possible an English-language certificate, which is a defined item on the national fee schedule rather than a favour. A border officer, and later your own doctor, both do better with a document than with a bag of unlabelled boxes. Our page on medical records covers what you are entitled to and what it costs.
Does Korea restrict what I take out?
For ordinary personal quantities of medicine and cosmetics, this is not usually where the friction is. The Korea Customs Service publishes guidance on departure procedures and declarable items, and it is worth a look if you are carrying anything unusual in volume. The far more common problem is arriving home with more than the destination allows.
