After you fly home: aftercare, follow-up, and going wrong at a distance

Short answer

What happens if there is a problem after I leave South Korea?

Follow-up is the part of treating abroad that nobody plans and everybody needs. Leave South Korea holding the clinic's Korean name and contact, an itemised receipt, a record of exactly what was used on you and by whom, your consent forms, and written aftercare. If a complication becomes a dispute, South Korea has a statutory agency that mediates outside the courts.

The gap nobody plans for

Treatment abroad has a structural problem that treatment at home does not. The person who treated you is in another country, and the person who sees you next was not there, did not choose the product, and has no record of what was done.

Almost everything below is about closing that gap before it opens, because the moment to collect information is while you are still standing in the clinic.

What to leave South Korea holding

Ask for these at the time. None of them are unusual requests in a clinic doing things properly.

  • The clinic's full Korean name and contact details. The Korean name is what any later search, register lookup, or formal step will need.
  • An itemised receipt. Not a total — the items.
  • Exactly what was used on you. The product's Korean name, the dose or the device settings, and who performed it.
  • Your signed consent forms, in a language you can read.
  • Written aftercare, including which symptoms mean contact a doctor now rather than wait.

That last item is the one most often left as spoken advice through an interpreter, which is to say left as nothing.

Establish the contact route before you need it

Before you leave, ask three questions and note the answers:

  1. Who handles a problem — the coordinator, the doctor, a general inbox?
  2. In what language?
  3. How quickly do they reply?

"Message the coordinator on KakaoTalk, replies same day" is a plan. A vague answer is also information, and it is much better to have it before you have paid than after something is not settling.

While you are still in the country, the 1330 helpline — 1330 locally, +82-2-1330 from abroad — answers in eight languages and can interpret a call for you, which is useful when the clinic's English-speaking coordinator is not on shift.

When you are home and something is not right

See a doctor where you live. That is not a lesser option; it is the correct one, and it is why the record matters so much. A local doctor can act far faster when told the exact product, dose, and date than when told "a filler, in Seoul, in March".

Give them the itemised record, the product names, and the written aftercare. If the treatment involved an injectable, the specific product matters — some can be dissolved and others cannot, and that decision is made faster with a name than with a description.

If it becomes a dispute

A complication is not automatically a dispute. But if it becomes one, South Korea has a statutory route that does not require going to court.

The Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency mediates and arbitrates medical disputes. The government's plain-language guidance frames eligibility as being a party to the dispute or their representative, and states no nationality condition. An arbitration decision carries the same effect as a final court judgment, which is why choosing arbitration is a decision to be bound rather than a step to try.

The published material does not settle how proceedings run for someone who has already left the country. If a problem has appeared and you are still in South Korea, that is an argument for contacting the counselling centre before you fly rather than after.

Counselling centre: +82-2-1670-2545 or +82-2-6210-0080, kmedi@k-medi.or.kr.

Why the registration check mattered

An institution registered to treat foreign patients is required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance. That single condition is what turns a claim from a moral argument into a practical one.

It is also the reason this site keeps saying to confirm registration before paying a deposit. The check takes minutes when you are choosing. It is worth very little once you need it.

The bottom line

Collect the record while you are still in the clinic: Korean name and contact, itemised receipt, product and dose, who performed it, consent forms, written aftercare. Agree who you contact about a problem, in what language, and how fast. See a local doctor if something goes wrong, and hand them the specifics. And if it becomes a dispute, South Korea's statutory agency mediates outside the courts, with no nationality condition stated in the guidance.

Frequently asked questions

What records should I take home?
The clinic's full Korean name and contact details, an itemised receipt, exactly what was used on you — product, dose or settings, and the person who performed it — your signed consent forms, and written aftercare instructions. Ask at the time. Reconstructing any of it by email from another country, in another language, is far harder.
Who treats a complication once I am home?
A doctor where you live, who was not present and did not choose the product. That is precisely why the record matters: a local doctor can act much faster when told the exact product and dose than when told "a filler, in Seoul, in March".
Can I contact the Korean clinic from abroad?
Usually, and it is worth establishing how before you need it. Ask before you leave who handles problems, in what language, and how quickly they reply — an answer of "message the coordinator on KakaoTalk" is a plan; a shrug is also information, delivered early.
Can I use the Korean dispute agency if I have left the country?
The government's plain-language guidance frames eligibility as being a party to the dispute or their representative, and states no nationality condition. What the published pages do not settle is how proceedings run for someone who has already left, so ask the agency's counselling centre — and ideally ask before you fly, if a problem has already appeared.
Is the clinic insured?
An institution registered to treat foreign patients is required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance. That requirement is what makes a claim worth pursuing, and it is the strongest argument for checking registration before treatment rather than after a problem.
What if the problem is with the agency rather than the clinic?
The dispute agency handles medical disputes. A complaint purely about an intermediary's fees or conduct is a different matter, and the first question there is whether that intermediary was registered under the foreign-patient scheme at all.
