1e:T1484,<h2 id="combining-is-the-normal-case">Combining is the normal case</h2>
<p>It is worth saying plainly, because the question is usually asked nervously: having more than one
thing done is standard practice in facial aesthetics, not a shortcut invented for travellers.</p>
<p>In 2016 fifteen specialists published consensus recommendations on combining botulinum toxin,
fillers and energy-based devices, developed by voting with a threshold of 75% agreement. The
existence of that document tells you the combination question was settled some time ago. What the
document actually says is more useful than the fact of it.</p>
<h2 id="what-can-share-a-single-session">What can share a single session</h2>
<p>Two specifics from that consensus are worth carrying into a consultation.</p>
<p><strong>Toxin and filler, either order.</strong> They may be performed together, and the consensus does not
prefer one sequence over the other. This is the most common pairing in the field.</p>
<p><strong>Ultrasound before injectables.</strong> Where microfocused ultrasound is combined with injectables on
the same day, the ultrasound comes first. There is a logic to it that generalises: energy delivered
into tissue is easier to aim before that tissue has been changed by an injection.</p>
<p>For laser with toxin there is a separate and unusually concrete piece of evidence. A retrospective
review looked at 616 same-day treatments across 393 people, combining botulinum toxin at the
glabella and around the eyes with full-face nonablative fractional laser. Eyelid ptosis appeared
once, a rate of 0.2%, and the authors concluded the risk of the toxin reaching an unintended muscle
was minimal in that setting.</p>
<p>Read the detail before generalising it. Those were nonablative fractional devices at 1,927 nm and
1,550 nm. It is a finding about that pairing, not a licence to combine anything with anything.</p>
<h2 id="the-interval-and-why-it-exists">The interval, and why it exists</h2>
<p>The number that shapes a trip is one to two weeks. That is the consensus spacing between
consecutive treatments, and the reasoning has two parts.</p>
<p>The first is that side effects from the previous step need time to resolve. The second is the one
people skip: until a treatment has settled you cannot tell what it achieved, and a clinician
choosing the next step without that information is guessing.</p>
<p>So the interval is not a safety buffer alone. It is the feedback loop that makes a course a course
rather than a sequence of hopeful appointments.</p>
<h2 id="where-the-interval-is-not-negotiable">Where the interval is not negotiable</h2>
<p>Most intervals bend a little. One in this field does not, and it is worth knowing because the
treatment involved is sold aggressively in Korea by the package.</p>
<p>Laser toning given three times a week for two months left every one of 23 patients with visible
mottled loss of pigment, and none of the thirteen followed for four further months recovered it.
The author's conclusion was that toning should not be repeated more often than once every two
weeks.</p>
<p>Our page on <a href="/concerns/laser-on-darker-skin/">laser and darker skin</a> covers that evidence in full.
The relevance here is simple: a ten-session course of anything, compressed into one week because
that is the length of the holiday, is the shape of plan that produced that result.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-fits-in-a-short-trip">What actually fits in a short trip</h2>
<p>Put the two facts together and the planning answer falls out.</p>
<p><strong>One appointment carrying a combination: yes.</strong> A well-chosen pairing in a single session is
ordinary practice and well documented.</p>
<p><strong>A course of the same treatment: no.</strong> The interval that makes a course work is longer than most
trips, so compressing it does not deliver the course faster, it delivers a different and worse
treatment.</p>
<p><strong>Two trips: often the honest answer.</strong> For anything meant to run as a series, taking the first
session properly and scheduling the rest against the interval beats fitting four sessions around a
return flight.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-when-a-package-is-proposed">What to ask when a package is proposed</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which of these are you combining in one session, and in what order?</strong> A clinic with a plan can
answer in one sentence.</li>
<li><strong>What interval does this course actually need?</strong> Then compare that number to your dates rather
than to the package price.</li>
<li><strong>What will you assess before the next session, and when?</strong> If nothing is being assessed between
sessions, the interval is not doing its job.</li>
<li><strong>Can the remainder be done at home or on a later trip?</strong> For several of these treatments the
answer is yes, and it changes what you need to buy now.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>You can have more than one thing done, and often in the same appointment, with a consensus document
and a 393-patient safety series behind the common combinations.</p>
