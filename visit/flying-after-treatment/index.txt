22:T14c7,<h2 id="why-aviation-has-waiting-periods-at-all">Why aviation has waiting periods at all</h2>
<p>Start with the reason the rules exist, because it explains why so few of them apply here.</p>
<p>Gas expands as pressure drops. Intestinal gas expands by roughly 30% at a cabin altitude of 8,000
feet, and the UK Civil Aviation Authority's guidance for health professionals is built around that
one fact. It asks for ten days after abdominal surgery. Around 24 hours after a laparoscopic
procedure, because carbon dioxide is left in the abdomen. Around seven days after neurosurgery,
because gas may be trapped inside the skull. Up to six weeks after retinal surgery, depending on
which gas was used.</p>
<p>Every one of those is about a pocket of gas or a closed cavity.</p>
<p>Read the same guidance looking for lasers, injectables or peels and there is nothing, because these
treatments leave no cavity and trap no gas. That silence is the answer to the safety version of the
question.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-cabin-actually-does-to-skin">What a cabin actually does to skin</h2>
<p>Two conditions matter, and neither is dramatic.</p>
<p><strong>Pressure.</strong> A cabin sits at the equivalent of 5,000 to 8,000 feet, which the authority describes
as causing a slight reduction in oxygen. Enough to notice if you have a lung or heart condition.
Not enough to undo a treatment.</p>
<p><strong>Humidity.</strong> Cabin air runs at about 20% humidity against 40 to 60% in an office. This is the one
you feel. Water leaves the outer layer of the skin faster than it is replaced, and skin that has
just been treated is already short of its usual barrier, so it reads as tight, flat and a little
red.</p>
<p>That is a comfort problem with a simple answer: an occlusive moisturiser, applied before boarding
and again during the flight.</p>
<h2 id="the-dehydration-myth-corrected">The dehydration myth, corrected</h2>
<p>Almost every article on this subject tells you to drink water so your skin does not dehydrate on
the plane. It is worth separating what is true from what is not.</p>
<p>The authority's own figure is that an eight-hour flight costs you about 150 ml of fluid, roughly a
small cup, and it states plainly that there is no risk of dehydration from this.</p>
<p>So your body is not dehydrated after a flight. Your skin surface is genuinely drier, because that
is a local effect of low humidity on the outermost layer. Those are different problems with
different fixes. The first needs nothing. The second needs moisturiser, not litres of water.</p>
<h2 id="the-constraint-that-is-real-how-you-will-look">The constraint that is real: how you will look</h2>
<p>Once safety is off the table, what remains is appearance, and that is a scheduling question rather
than a medical one.</p>
<p>Swelling and bruising are most visible in the first days after treatment. Sitting upright for a
long flight is not going to improve either. If you have photographs, a wedding or a first day back
at work waiting for you, the timing of the appointment inside the trip is what decides how that
goes.</p>
<p>Clinic advice on intervals varies by procedure and by clinic, and since aviation guidance does not
cover these treatments, the clinic's own figure is the one that governs. Ask for it at the
consultation and get it in writing.</p>
<h2 id="the-one-to-take-seriously-sun-afterwards">The one to take seriously: sun afterwards</h2>
<p>If any part of this page deserves weight, it is this, and it matters most to anyone pairing Korea
with a beach.</p>
<p>Sunscreen was the only measure that consistently prevented post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in a
2025 systematic review of 369 patients with skin of colour, and laser was what triggered 95% of
the cases in it. Freshly treated skin is exactly the skin that marks.</p>
<p>A week in Korea followed by a week somewhere sunny is an extremely common itinerary, and it is the
one combination on this page worth reordering if your clinic's sun interval does not fit it. Our
page on <a href="/concerns/laser-on-darker-skin/">laser and darker skin</a> covers the pigment side in full.</p>
<h2 id="where-to-put-the-appointment-in-your-trip">Where to put the appointment in your trip</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Early in the trip</strong> gives redness and swelling time to settle before you fly, and it keeps you
in the country while anything that needs review is still reviewable.</li>
<li><strong>Late in the trip</strong> protects your photographs on the earlier days and sends you home near the
peak of any bruising.</li>
<li><strong>Split across two trips</strong> is the honest answer for any course that wants real intervals between
sessions, rather than compressing them to fit one set of flights.</li>
</ul>
<p>Most people who have done it both ways prefer early, and they usually say it was the follow-up
rather than the swelling that changed their mind.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>There is no aviation rule keeping you on the ground after a skin treatment, and the reason is
structural: those rules were written about trapped gas, and a laser or an injection leaves none.</p>
