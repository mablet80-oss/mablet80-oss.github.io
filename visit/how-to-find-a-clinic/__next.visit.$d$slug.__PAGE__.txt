1f:T145b,<h2 id="what-this-page-will-not-do">What this page will not do</h2>
<p>It will not name a clinic, rank clinics, or send you to one. That is a standing rule here and there
is a reason for it beyond principle: a publisher paid per referral has an interest in where you go,
and no disclosure is strong enough to cancel that interest out.</p>
<p>What is useful, and what nobody seems to write down, is <strong>how to look</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="search-where-koreans-search">Search where Koreans search</h2>
<p>English-language results are not a neutral view of the market. They are weighted towards clinics
that market to foreigners — which is a legitimate business, and a different set from the clinics
Koreans themselves use.</p>
<p>The practical move is to search the <strong>Korean name</strong> of the treatment you are considering and read
the results with a translation tool. Every treatment page on this site lists the Korean terms for
exactly this reason. You will see a wider field, and you will see what clinics say to a domestic
audience rather than to a visiting one.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-clinics-name-tells-you">What the clinic's name tells you</h2>
<p>This is the signal a foreign visitor has no way to read, and it comes from the profession's own
body rather than from us.</p>
<p>Under the Medical Service Act, only a properly licensed institution may use <strong>피부과</strong> —
dermatology — in its name. The Korean Academy of Dermatology warns that names shaped like
<strong>○○스킨클리닉</strong> or <strong>○○피부&#x26;에스테틱</strong> may indicate a doctor who is <strong>not</strong> a board-certified
dermatologist.</p>
<p>Be careful about what that does and does not mean, because it is easy to overstate:</p>
<ul>
<li>It is <strong>not</strong> about legality. <strong>Any doctor holding a Korean medical licence may perform these
procedures.</strong> Board certification in dermatology is a specialty qualification, not a permission,
and a clinic run by a non-dermatologist is operating lawfully.</li>
<li>It <strong>is</strong> about knowing what you have. A board-certified dermatologist has completed medical
school, a one-year internship, <strong>four years</strong> of dermatology training, and the national board
examination.</li>
</ul>
<p>Whether that matters to you is your call. What should not happen is making that call without
knowing which you are in front of.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-a-doctor">How to check a doctor</h2>
<p>The Korean Academy of Dermatology publishes a search for the official <strong>피부과 전문의</strong> mark, and a
directory of certified dermatologists by area. The certificate is also displayed in the clinic — ask
to see it.</p>
<p>Separately, and for a different purpose, confirm the clinic is <strong>registered to treat foreign
patients</strong>. That registration carries a medical malpractice liability insurance requirement, which
is the condition that matters if anything goes wrong.</p>
<p>Two checks, two different questions: <em>what kind of doctor is this</em> and <em>can this clinic lawfully
treat me and is it insured to</em>.</p>
<h2 id="the-claims-that-should-end-the-conversation">The claims that should end the conversation</h2>
<p>The association is explicit about advertising that crosses the line, and names the pattern:
<strong>과장된 표현</strong> — exaggerated expressions. Guarantees of results. "100% effective."</p>
<p>Those are not enthusiasm. They are claims the profession's own body says violate the Medical Service
Act. A clinic willing to break an advertising rule to get you through the door has told you
something about how it treats rules in general, and you learned it before paying anything.</p>
<p>Alongside that, treat these as questions rather than disqualifications: before-and-after galleries
presented as typical, a package quoted before anyone has looked at your skin, and a discount that
expires if you do not decide today.</p>
<h2 id="ranking-sites-apps-and-agencies">Ranking sites, apps, and agencies</h2>
<p>There are a great many, and some are useful. The question that sorts them is simple: <strong>who pays
them, and how much of what I pay ends up there?</strong></p>
<p>The Medical Service Act prohibits introducing, referring or soliciting patients for profit, with
limited exceptions, and agencies bringing foreign patients to South Korea must register under a
separate statute to operate lawfully. So an intermediary is not automatically a problem — an
unregistered one taking a share of your treatment fee is.</p>
<p>Ask any agency for its registration, and ask directly whether it takes a commission on what you pay.
The answer, and the manner of it, is information.</p>
