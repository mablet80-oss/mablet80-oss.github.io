20:T1662,<h2 id="what-you-actually-need">What you actually need</h2>
<p>Less than most people expect. Aesthetic treatment in South Korea is <strong>non-covered care</strong> — national
health insurance pays nothing towards it for anybody, resident or visitor. That sounds like a
disadvantage and is quietly the opposite: because there is no insurance to be enrolled in, there is
no residency requirement, no Korean identification number, and no local insurance to arrange.</p>
<p>What you need is a passport, an appointment, and a way to pay.</p>
<p>You may also need nothing more than your normal entry permission. For a day procedure — a toxin
injection, a laser session, a booster — many nationalities enter visa-free or on an ordinary
short-term visa and are treated during that stay. The medical visa categories exist for treatment
that needs a longer stay, for a caregiver, or where a clinic is inviting you formally.</p>
<h2 id="who-will-interpret">Who will interpret</h2>
<p>This is the question most guides skip, and it shapes the whole visit.</p>
<p>At even well-regarded clinics it is uncommon for the treating doctor to be fluent in your language.
What clinics set up for international patients provide instead is a <strong>coordinator</strong> who interprets.
The difference between a good and a poor experience is usually how far that coordinator goes: one
who stays through the consultation and the procedure, translating the doctor's actual assessment and
the aftercare instructions, versus one who books you in and hands you over.</p>
<p>So ask, before you book: <strong>who interprets, and are they present for the procedure as well as the
consultation?</strong></p>
<p>If you need a language line independently of the clinic, the Korea Tourism Organization runs the
<strong>1330 helpline</strong> — 1330 inside South Korea, +82-2-1330 from abroad — answering in Korean, English,
Japanese, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese, Thai and Malay, and able to interpret a call for you.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-settle-in-writing-before-you-pay-a-deposit">What to settle in writing before you pay a deposit</h2>
<p>Conversation in a consultation room, through an interpreter, is not a record. Get these in writing:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which product and how much.</strong> The Korean product name, the dose or number of shots. It is also
what you need to look the product up.</li>
<li><strong>Who performs it.</strong> Not the clinic — the person.</li>
<li><strong>What the quote includes.</strong> Itemised: consultation, anaesthetic, aftercare products, any
follow-up visit.</li>
<li><strong>Cancellation terms</strong>, if you are paying a deposit before you travel.</li>
<li><strong>Aftercare</strong>, and who to contact with a problem once you are home.</li>
</ul>
<p>A clinic that will put those in an email before you arrive is telling you something useful. So is
one that will not.</p>
<h2 id="paying-and-the-rule-that-protects-you">Paying, and the rule that protects you</h2>
<p>Because aesthetic treatment is non-covered, each clinic sets its own prices, and they vary widely.
But the Medical Service Act is direct about one thing:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>No founder of a medical institution may collect fees and charges in excess of an amount notified
or posted.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>So the posted list is a ceiling, not decoration. If a quote comes in above it, the question to ask
is which posted item the quote corresponds to — a quote can legitimately be higher because it covers
more items, which is a different thing from charging more for the same one.</p>
<p>Ask for an <strong>itemised receipt</strong> and keep it. It is the document that makes anything afterwards
possible.</p>
<h2 id="before-you-book-the-one-check-worth-doing">Before you book: the one check worth doing</h2>
<p>A clinic may not treat foreign patients as a business unless it is <strong>registered</strong> to do so, and
registered institutions are required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance. That
insurance requirement is what makes a claim practical if something goes wrong, which is why the
check belongs before the deposit rather than after the problem.</p>
<p>Ask to see the registration certificate, and confirm the clinic's Korean name against the published
register.</p>
<h2 id="after-and-getting-home">After, and getting home</h2>
<p>There is no single answer to how soon you can fly, because it depends on what was done. Ask, and get
the answer in writing.</p>
<p>The planning rule that holds regardless: <strong>do not schedule treatment for the day before you
travel.</strong> Swelling and bruising are normal after injectables, downtime after energy devices and
resurfacing is real, and you want room in your trip to be seen again if something is not settling.</p>
<p>Leave South Korea with the clinic's full Korean name and contact, the itemised receipt, a record of
what was used on you and by whom, your consent forms, and written aftercare. If a problem develops
later, that folder is the difference between a conversation and a shrug.</p>
<p>And if it becomes a dispute rather than a complication, South Korea has a statutory agency that
mediates medical disputes outside the courts, with outcomes that carry real legal weight.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>For self-pay skin treatment you need a passport and a way to pay, not residency or insurance, and
often no medical visa. Expect a coordinator rather than an English-speaking doctor, and confirm
before booking whether they stay for the procedure. Get the product, the dose, the operator, an
itemised quote and the aftercare in writing before you pay a deposit — and do not book treatment for
