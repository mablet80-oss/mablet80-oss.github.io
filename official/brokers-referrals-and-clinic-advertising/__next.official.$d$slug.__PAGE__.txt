20:T11fe,<h2 id="what-the-rule-is">What the rule is</h2>
<p>South Korea's Medical Service Act contains a provision that shapes the whole market a foreign patient
walks into, and almost no English-language guide mentions it. Article 27(3), in the government's
own English translation, begins:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>No one may introduce, make a referral of or solicit a patient to a medical institution or medical
personnel for profits, such as exempting or discounting medical expenses...</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The article then sets out limited exceptions.</p>
<p>Read plainly, this is a prohibition on the referral-fee business model as a default. It is not a
rule about advertising standards or disclosure. It addresses the act of steering a patient to a
particular doctor or clinic for gain — and it names discounting the patient's medical expenses as
an example of the inducement it has in mind, which is worth remembering the next time a platform
offers you money off for booking through it.</p>
<h2 id="who-it-applies-to-and-the-exception-that-matters">Who it applies to, and the exception that matters</h2>
<p>"No one" is broad, and it is meant to be. But South Korea plainly does permit agencies to bring
international patients to Korean clinics: there is a registration scheme for exactly that, and
thousands of registered inducement businesses operating under it.</p>
<p>The two facts fit together. The general prohibition sits in the Medical Service Act; foreign-patient
attraction is separately regulated under the Act on Support for Overseas Expansion of Healthcare
System and Attraction of Foreign Patients, which requires agencies to register in their own
category. Registration is what brings an intermediary inside the lawful route.</p>
<p>So the question to ask about an agency is not whether agencies are allowed. It is whether <em>this</em>
agency is registered.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-it-yourself">How to check it yourself</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read the provision.</strong> The Korean government publishes an official English translation of the
Medical Service Act. Read Article 27 rather than a summary — including this one.</li>
<li><strong>Check the agency's registration.</strong> Registered inducement businesses appear in the same
published system as registered clinics. Ask the agency for its registration and search the
register for it.</li>
<li><strong>Ask who pays whom.</strong> A straightforward question — "does the clinic pay you a commission, and is
it a percentage of what I pay?" — is not rude, and the answer tells you what you are dealing
with. Money paid to an intermediary is not conjured from nowhere. It comes out of the price you
are quoted, or out of what the clinic can afford to spend on your care.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-this-does-not-settle">What this does not settle</h2>
<p>This page is about the referral relationship, not about medical advertising, which the Act also
regulates. We have not reproduced those provisions here because we have not yet read the
advertising articles in the official English text, and we do not paraphrase statutes we have not
read. That section will be added when it is sourced properly.</p>
<p>Nor does any of this tell you whether a registered agency is a good one. Registration means it is
operating inside the scheme. It is not a quality assessment, and it does not mean the clinic at the
other end of the introduction is the right clinic for you.</p>
<h2 id="why-this-site-works-the-way-it-does">Why this site works the way it does</h2>
<p>It is worth saying plainly, because it explains an absence readers may notice. This site carries no
booking links, no clinic recommendations, and no referral arrangements. Part of the reason is the
law above. The larger part is that we cannot be a useful reference and a paid intermediary at the
same time. Any disclosure we could write would be weaker than the incentive it disclosed.</p>
<p>Where a page ever carries paid placement, it will be labelled as such on the page itself. That is
the whole of our commercial model, and it is why we can tell you to ask an agency who pays it.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>South Korea's Medical Service Act prohibits introducing, referring or soliciting patients for profit,
naming discounted medical fees as an example of the inducement it covers, with limited exceptions.
Agencies bringing foreign patients to South Korea operate lawfully by registering under the separate
foreign-patient statute — so check that an intermediary is registered, and ask directly whether it
