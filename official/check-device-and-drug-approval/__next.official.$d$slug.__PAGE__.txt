21:Tfa6,<h2 id="what-the-rule-is">What the rule is</h2>
<p>Nothing may be sold and used as a medical device or a medicine in South Korea without a licence from the
Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. That covers the equipment used in aesthetic clinics — the
ultrasound and radiofrequency platforms, the lasers, the microneedling systems — and it covers the
injectables, from botulinum toxins to the polynucleotide and polymer products South Korea is known for.</p>
<p>The useful part for a patient is that the ministry publishes it. Approval is not a claim a clinic
makes about itself. It is a record held by the regulator, in a public system, that you can look up.</p>
<h2 id="what-approval-actually-covers">What approval actually covers</h2>
<p>A licence attaches to a specific product, under a specific name, in a specific classification, for
specific indications. All four of those matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The product.</strong> Not the treatment, not the clinic's name for the treatment. Korean clinics often
market a procedure under a house brand that does not appear on any licence.</li>
<li><strong>The name.</strong> The licence is issued under the Korean product name. That is what you search.</li>
<li><strong>The classification.</strong> Devices are classified by risk, and the class tells you how heavily the
product was scrutinised.</li>
<li><strong>The indications.</strong> What the product was approved to be used <em>for</em>, which is not always what it
is being offered to you for.</li>
</ul>
<p>The ministry also publishes the other half of the record: safety letters, recalls, and suspension
orders against products already on the market. A product having been approved once is not the same
as it being in good standing today.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-it-yourself">How to check it yourself</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Get the Korean product name in writing.</strong> Ask the clinic which product and which device model
will be used. A clinic that will not put this in an email before you travel has told you
something useful.</li>
<li><strong>Search the right portal.</strong> Devices — lasers, ultrasound, radiofrequency — are on the ministry's
device information portal. Drugs and biologics, including toxins and injectables, are on the drug
safety portal. Both are linked below.</li>
<li><strong>Read the indications, not just the fact of approval.</strong> The question is whether the product is
licensed for what is being proposed for you.</li>
<li><strong>Check the product is still in good standing.</strong> Look for recent safety notices on the product
or its manufacturer.</li>
</ol>
<p>Both services are in Korean. That is a real obstacle, and the honest workaround is the Korean
product name plus a translation tool, not a guess based on the English brand.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-does-not-tell-you">What it does not tell you</h2>
<p>Approval is a floor, and it is easy to oversell. It does not tell you:</p>
<ul>
<li>that the treatment will work on your skin, or how well;</li>
<li>that this clinic is using the product at the approved dose, setting, or interval;</li>
<li>that the person operating the device has the training to do it well; or</li>
<li>that the product in the room is the product on the invoice.</li>
</ul>
<p>That last one is why the practical version of this check is not only "is it approved" but "show me
what you are using". Asking to see the box, the vial, or the lot number before an injectable is
used is normal in a clinic that is doing things properly.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>South Korea's medical regulator publishes what it has approved, and you can look up a device or an
injectable before you agree to it. Get the Korean product name in writing, search the ministry's
device or drug portal, and read what the product was licensed for rather than stopping at the fact
