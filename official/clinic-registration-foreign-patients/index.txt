21:T1367,<h2 id="what-the-rule-is">What the rule is</h2>
<p>South Korea does not leave the treatment of foreign patients to the open market. Under the Act on
Support for Overseas Expansion of Healthcare System and Attraction of Foreign Patients — usually
shortened to 의료해외진출법 — a hospital or clinic must register with its provincial or metropolitan
government before it may seek out and treat patients from abroad as a business. The Ministry of
Health and Welfare oversees the scheme, and the Korea Health Industry Development Institute runs the
online system where institutions apply and where the register is published.</p>
<p>The Act creates a second, separate category for the businesses that introduce patients to clinics:
agencies, brokers, and the intermediaries a patient often meets first. They register too. That
pairing is the point of the scheme. Regulating only the clinic would leave the part of the chain
where most of the trouble happens untouched.</p>
<p>One condition attached to registration matters more than the rest. Since 2016, registered
institutions have been required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance; the Act also
accepts membership of the medical liability mutual-aid association in its place. Medical Korea
reports overall compliance with that requirement as "100% Insured", alongside figures of more than
3,800 registered medical institutions and more than 2,300 registered inducement businesses as of
August 2023.</p>
<h2 id="who-it-applies-to">Who it applies to</h2>
<p>The scheme covers institutions treating patients who are neither Korean nationals nor covered by
Korean national health insurance — in practice, the international patient this site is written
for. It does not change how a clinic treats domestic patients, and it is not a specialist
qualification: a registered institution may be a large hospital or a single-doctor skin clinic.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-it-yourself">How to check it yourself</h2>
<p>Medical Korea's own instruction to patients is direct, and worth quoting as it appears: <em>"always
confirm that your clinic is a registered medical clinic for foreign patients"</em>, and <em>"CHECK THE
REGISTRATION CERTIFICATE!"</em> — published under the banner <em>"TRANSPARENT HEALTH SYSTEM WITH NO
ILLEGAL BROKERS."</em></p>
<p>There are two ways to do it, and they are worth doing in this order.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ask the clinic to show the certificate.</strong> Do this before you pay a deposit, by email if you are
booking from abroad. A clinic that is registered will not find the question strange.</li>
<li><strong>Search the register.</strong> The Foreign Patient Attraction Information System publishes the status
of registered institutions, and the underlying dataset is on South Korea's open data portal. Both are
Korean-language, so search the clinic's Korean name — which is the name on the certificate, and
which you should ask for anyway.</li>
</ul>
<p>If an agency contacted you rather than the clinic, check the agency as well. It is registered under
the same Act, in its own category.</p>
<h2 id="what-registration-does-not-tell-you">What registration does not tell you</h2>
<p>This is where most guides overstate things, so it is worth being plain. Registration is
administrative permission with conditions, not a quality assessment. It tells you the institution
is a licensed medical institution that has met the conditions of the scheme, including insurance.
It does not tell you:</p>
<ul>
<li>which doctor will actually perform your procedure, or their specialty and training;</li>
<li>whether the device or product used on you is approved in South Korea for what it is being used for;</li>
<li>whether the price you have been quoted resembles what the clinic charges domestic patients; or</li>
<li>anything about outcomes.</li>
</ul>
<p>South Korea has a second, stricter tier above registration for institutions assessed on foreign-patient
management and patient safety. Registration is the floor. Accreditation is the signal.</p>
<h2 id="if-something-is-wrong">If something is wrong</h2>
<p>If you have a dispute with a Korean clinic, there is a statutory body outside the courts that
handles medical disputes, and its decisions carry real legal weight. The insurance condition
attached to registration is what makes a claim against a registered institution practical, which
is the strongest single argument for checking the register before you book rather than after.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>A Korean clinic must be registered before it seeks out and treats foreign patients as a business, and so must any
agency that introduces you to one. Ask to see the registration certificate before you pay anything,
then confirm the clinic's Korean name against the published register. Registration proves the
clinic met the conditions of the scheme, including malpractice insurance — it does not prove the
