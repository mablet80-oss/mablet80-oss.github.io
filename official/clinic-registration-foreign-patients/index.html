Is your Korean clinic registered to treat foreign patients?

Korean law requires clinics and agencies to register before treating foreign patients. How the register works, and how to check a clinic against it.

Short answer

How do I check whether a Korean clinic is registered to treat foreign patients?

A clinic in South Korea may not treat foreign patients as a business unless it is registered as a foreign patient attraction institution, and agencies that recruit patients must register separately. Registration is administered by the Korea Health Industry Development Institute for the Ministry of Health and Welfare, and registered institutions are required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance. The register is published, and Medical Korea's own advice to patients is to ask the clinic to show its registration certificate.

What the rule is

South Korea does not leave the treatment of foreign patients to the open market. Under the Act on Support for Overseas Expansion of Healthcare System and Attraction of Foreign Patients — usually shortened to 의료해외진출법 — a hospital or clinic must register before it may seek out and treat patients from abroad as a business. The registration is administered by the Korea Health Industry Development Institute on behalf of the Ministry of Health and Welfare.

The Act creates a second, separate category for the businesses that introduce patients to clinics: agencies, brokers, and the intermediaries a patient often meets first. They register too. That pairing is the point of the scheme. Regulating only the clinic would leave the part of the chain where most of the trouble happens untouched.

One condition attached to registration matters more than the rest. Since 2016, registered institutions have been required to carry medical malpractice liability insurance. Medical Korea reports overall compliance with that requirement as "100% Insured", alongside figures of more than 3,800 registered medical institutions and more than 2,300 registered inducement businesses as of August 2023.

Who it applies to

The scheme covers institutions treating patients who are neither Korean nationals nor covered by Korean national health insurance — in practice, the international patient this site is written for. It does not change how a clinic treats domestic patients, and it is not a specialist qualification: a registered institution may be a large hospital or a single-doctor skin clinic.

How to check it yourself

Medical Korea's own instruction to patients is direct, and worth quoting as it appears: "always confirm that your clinic is a registered medical clinic for foreign patients", and "CHECK THE REGISTRATION CERTIFICATE!" — published under the banner "TRANSPARENT HEALTH SYSTEM WITH NO ILLEGAL BROKERS."

There are two ways to do it, and they are worth doing in this order.

  • Ask the clinic to show the certificate. Do this before you pay a deposit, by email if you are booking from abroad. A clinic that is registered will not find the question strange.
  • Search the register. The Foreign Patient Attraction Information System publishes the status of registered institutions, and the underlying dataset is on South Korea's open data portal. Both are Korean-language, so search the clinic's Korean name — which is the name on the certificate, and which you should ask for anyway.

If an agency contacted you rather than the clinic, check the agency as well. It is registered under the same Act, in its own category.

What registration does not tell you

This is where most guides overstate things, so it is worth being plain. Registration is administrative permission with conditions, not a quality assessment. It tells you the institution is a licensed medical institution that has met the conditions of the scheme, including insurance. It does not tell you:

  • which doctor will actually perform your procedure, or their specialty and training;
  • whether the device or product used on you is approved in South Korea for what it is being used for;
  • whether the price you have been quoted resembles what the clinic charges domestic patients; or
  • anything about outcomes.

South Korea has a second, stricter tier above registration for institutions assessed on foreign-patient management and patient safety. Registration is the floor. Accreditation is the signal.

If something is wrong

If you have a dispute with a Korean clinic, there is a statutory body outside the courts that handles medical disputes, and its decisions carry real legal weight. The insurance condition attached to registration is what makes a claim against a registered institution practical, which is the strongest single argument for checking the register before you book rather than after.

The bottom line

A Korean clinic must be registered before it treats foreign patients as a business, and so must any agency that introduces you to one. Ask to see the registration certificate before you pay anything, then confirm the clinic's Korean name against the published register. Registration proves the clinic met the conditions of the scheme, including malpractice insurance — it does not prove the clinic is a good one.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is a "foreign patient attraction institution"?
It is the legal category for a hospital or clinic permitted to seek out and treat patients who are not Korean nationals or residents. The same Act creates a second category for the agencies that introduce those patients, and both must register.
Does registration mean the clinic is good?
No. Registration is an administrative permission with conditions attached, chiefly that the institution is a licensed medical institution and carries malpractice liability insurance. It is a floor, not a ranking, and it says nothing about a particular doctor's training or about the device being used on you.
What happens if a clinic is not registered?
Treating foreign patients as a business without registration is outside the scheme the Act sets up. For you as a patient the practical problem is narrower and more immediate: an unregistered institution has not had to satisfy the insurance condition, which is the condition that matters if something goes wrong.
Is the register available in English?
Medical Korea publishes English pages explaining the system, but the searchable register itself is Korean-language. The dataset of registered institutions is also published on the national open data portal.
The clinic showed me a certificate. Is that enough?
It is what Medical Korea tells patients to ask for, and it is a reasonable first step. A certificate can be out of date, so treat it as a prompt to search the register for the institution's name rather than as the end of the check.
