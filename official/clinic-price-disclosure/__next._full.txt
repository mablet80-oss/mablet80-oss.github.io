22:T1069,<h2 id="what-the-rule-is">What the rule is</h2>
<p>Almost everything this site covers — botulinum toxin, lasers, skin boosters, lifting devices — is
<strong>비급여</strong> care in South Korea: non-covered. National health insurance pays nothing towards it, and each
institution sets its own price. That is why the same treatment can cost twice as much one street
over, and why "what does it cost in South Korea" has no single answer.</p>
<p>South Korea's response to that is not price control. It is two disclosure duties in the Medical Service
Act, and the second one is more useful to a patient than most people realise.</p>
<p><strong>Article 45</strong> requires the founder of a medical institution to notify its non-covered medical
expenses in a way that lets "patients or his/her guardians easily understand them", and to post the
charges it collects for issuing certificates. It then adds the sentence that matters:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>No founder of a medical institution may collect fees and charges in excess of an amount notified
or posted.</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>Article 45-2</strong> requires institutions to report those non-covered prices to the Ministry of Health
and Welfare, and empowers the Minister to investigate, analyse and disclose the resulting picture.</p>
<h2 id="who-it-applies-to-and-how-often">Who it applies to, and how often</h2>
<p>The Ministry's standard for that reporting — Notice No. 2023-168, a full revision issued on
4 September 2023 — sets different rhythms by institution size: <strong>hospital-level institutions report
twice a year, clinic-level institutions once a year</strong>. The reporting and disclosure work is
delegated to the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service and the National Health Insurance
Service.</p>
<p>That distinction matters for this audience. The skin clinic you are considering is almost certainly
clinic-level, which means the published figure for it may be up to a year old.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-check-it-yourself">How to check it yourself</h2>
<p>Two checks, and they do different jobs.</p>
<p><strong>In the clinic, check the posted list.</strong> The institution has to make its non-covered charges
understandable to you, and it cannot collect more than what it has notified or posted. So the
posted list is not decoration — it is the ceiling. If a quote comes in above it, ask which posted
item the quote corresponds to.</p>
<p><strong>Before you travel, check the published data.</strong> HIRA publishes the reported prices, and the same
data is available through the national open data portal. Both are Korean-language, so search the
clinic's Korean name. Use it for the order of magnitude — is this quote in the same world as what
this clinic reports charging — rather than as a live price.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-does-not-tell-you">What it does not tell you</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>It is not a price control.</strong> A clinic may charge whatever it likes for a non-covered treatment.
The duty is to disclose it and then stick to it.</li>
<li><strong>It is not a fairness test.</strong> A posted price is not evidence that the price is reasonable, or
that a foreign patient is being quoted the same figure as a domestic one.</li>
<li><strong>It does not itemise your quote for you.</strong> Packages are where posted prices become hard to
compare, which is the practical reason to ask for a quote broken into items.</li>
<li><strong>It says nothing about who performs the treatment</strong>, or with what product — the questions that
determine whether the price is worth paying at all.</li>
</ul>

collecting more than the amount posted. Institutions also report those prices to the Ministry, and
the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service publishes them, annually for clinic-level
institutions. Ask for the posted list and an itemised quote, and treat any figure above the posted
