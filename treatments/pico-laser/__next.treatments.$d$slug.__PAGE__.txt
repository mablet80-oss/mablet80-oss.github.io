1d:T14da,<h2 id="what-a-picosecond-laser-is">What a picosecond laser is</h2>
<p>Laser treatment of pigment works by depositing energy into pigment faster than the surrounding
tissue can carry the heat away. The shorter the pulse, the more the effect is confined to the target.</p>
<p>A <strong>picosecond</strong> laser fires in pulses measured in trillionths of a second — around a thousand times
shorter than the nanosecond Q-switched lasers that preceded it. At that speed the dominant mechanism
shifts from heating to a <strong>photoacoustic</strong> one: pigment is shattered by pressure rather than cooked,
which in principle means less collateral heat in the surrounding skin.</p>
<p>That is a genuinely good principle, and it is the entire basis of how these devices are sold.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-pooled-trials-found-for-melasma">What the pooled trials found for melasma</h2>
<p>A <strong>2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in <em>Cureus</em></strong> pooled <strong>52 randomised controlled trials
covering 1,058 participants</strong>. For melasma, the headline result is not the one the marketing
predicts:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Picosecond lasers did not significantly outperform controls</strong> — mean difference <strong>−0.11</strong>
(95% CI −0.79 to 0.57).</li>
<li><strong>Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG at 1064 nm did</strong> — mean difference <strong>1.47</strong> (95% CI 0.08 to 2.85).</li>
<li>Across all laser types pooled together, the trend was <strong>not statistically significant</strong>
(MD 0.70; p = 0.2682).</li>
</ul>
<p>So in the pooled randomised evidence for melasma, the older and cheaper technology outperformed the
newer and more expensive one. The reviewers note that optimal picosecond parameters for different
wavelengths are still being refined — this is a "not yet demonstrated" rather than a "disproven" —
but the practical point for a patient stands: <strong>paying a premium for pico as a melasma treatment is
not currently supported by the pooled trials.</strong></p>
<h2 id="being-precise-about-what-this-does-and-does-not-say">Being precise about what this does and does not say</h2>
<p>Two cautions, because it would be easy to over-read this.</p>
<p><strong>It is about melasma.</strong> Pigmentation is not one condition. Melasma is a specific, chronic,
hormonally and sun-influenced pattern that recurs, and it is the hardest pigment problem there is.
Picosecond platforms have other uses, tattoo removal among them, that this analysis does not address.</p>
<p><strong>It is about the pooled average.</strong> Individual trials and individual patients vary, and device
settings differ enormously. What the meta-analysis rules out is a reliable across-the-board
advantage, not the possibility of benefit in a particular case.</p>
<p>The reviewers' own conclusion keeps that balance: lasers, <em>"particularly low-fluence QSNY lasers, are
effective adjunctive tools for melasma management. However, they should not be considered a panacea.
Optimal clinical management requires a multi-modal approach."</em> Adjunctive. Multi-modal. Not a cure.</p>
<h2 id="the-risk-that-matters-in-pigmented-skin">The risk that matters in pigmented skin</h2>
<p>The review reports no serious adverse events, with transient redness and localised burning common.
The complication to understand is <strong>pigmentary</strong>: post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and
hypopigmentation, occurring particularly at <strong>high fluences</strong>.</p>
<p>That is the whole reason "low-fluence" appears in the finding that did work. Treating pigment too
aggressively can provoke more pigment — the opposite of what you came for — and the risk is greater
in darker skin. Asking what fluence is being used, and why that choice for your skin type, is a
reasonable and revealing question.</p>
<h2 id="laser-toning-and-the-naming-problem">Laser toning, and the naming problem</h2>
<p>Korean clinics sell "laser toning", "dual toning", "pico toning" and similar. These are <strong>techniques</strong>
— repeated low-fluence passes across an area — not devices, and the same name may be delivered on a
Q-switched or a picosecond platform depending on the clinic.</p>
<p>Since the pooled evidence distinguishes by device and fluence rather than by marketing name, the
question that gets you real information is: which device, which wavelength, what fluence, how many
passes.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-a-korean-clinic">Questions to ask a Korean clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which device and wavelength, and what is its Korean product name?</li>
<li>What fluence, and why that setting for my skin type?</li>
<li>Is this melasma, or another kind of pigmentation? They are not managed the same way.</li>
<li>What is the plan if my pigmentation darkens after a session?</li>
<li>What happens if I stop — and what does maintenance look like?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Picosecond lasers shatter pigment with very short pulses, which is sound in principle. For melasma,
a 2026 meta-analysis of 52 randomised trials found they did not significantly outperform controls
while older low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG lasers did. Melasma is managed rather than cured, high
