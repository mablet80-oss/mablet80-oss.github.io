1e:T14ac,<h2 id="what-a-thread-lift-is">What a thread lift is</h2>
<p>A thread lift passes sutures under the skin and uses them to hold tissue in a higher position. The
threads carry small barbs or cones angled back towards the point they are anchored from, so tissue
slides one way along the thread and catches the other. Draw the thread up, and the tissue it has
caught comes with it.</p>
<p>The threads used now are <strong>absorbable</strong> — polydioxanone and similar materials — so the thread itself
is broken down by the body over months. That is the first thing to hold onto, because it sets up the
question that matters: if the thread is gone, what is holding the lift?</p>
<h2 id="what-it-is-for-and-what-it-is-not-for">What it is for, and what it is not for</h2>
<p>Thread lifting addresses <strong>position</strong>. Tissue that has descended — the mid-face, the jawline, the
upper neck — is physically moved and held.</p>
<p>It does not address skin quality, and it does not add volume. If the complaint is texture or tone,
this is the wrong treatment; if it is a hollow, filler is a different tool. And it is not surgery: it
repositions tissue on a suture rather than removing or resecting anything.</p>
<h2 id="how-long-it-lasts-from-the-published-follow-up">How long it lasts, from the published follow-up</h2>
<p>This is where marketing and evidence diverge most sharply, so the numbers here are given with the
study that produced them.</p>
<p>A 2017 review of facial thread lifting reports one series of <strong>117 patients followed for 12–24
months who maintained 70% of the initial correction</strong>, and <strong>96 patients followed for 36 months who
maintained 60%</strong>. So: it fades, gradually, and a meaningful part of what you paid for is gone within
a few years.</p>
<p>That same review's overall conclusion is unusually direct for this field, and worth reading as
written: <em>"this review suggests that it should not yet be adopted as an alternative to
rhytidectomy"</em> — rhytidectomy being a surgical facelift. It describes thread lift results as subtle
and of short duration by comparison.</p>
<p>A clinic quoting you a single confident duration is not quoting the literature.</p>

<p>The 2026 authors' conclusion is that thread lifting is "generally safe" but needs "careful patient
selection and technique mastery by practitioners". In other words, who does it matters.</p>
<h2 id="thread-lift-compared-with-the-energy-devices">Thread lift compared with the energy devices</h2>
<p>Korean clinics often present thread lifting alongside Ultherapy, Thermage, or InMode as though they
were interchangeable options for "lifting". They are not.</p>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>thread</strong> moves tissue mechanically. You see the change on the day.</li>
<li>An <strong>energy device</strong> heats tissue to provoke tightening over the following months. It moves
nothing, and the change is gradual and generally smaller.</li>
</ul>
<p>Someone proposing both should be able to say what each is doing in your case. If the answer is that
they go well together, ask what specifically each one is for.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-before-you-agree">Questions to ask before you agree</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which thread product and material, and what is its Korean name? Look it up on the device portal.</li>
<li>How many threads, placed where, and anchored to what?</li>
<li>What is your plan if a thread becomes visible, palpable, or extrudes?</li>
<li>What happens if that occurs after I have flown home — who treats it, and at whose cost?</li>
<li>Are you proposing this instead of, or as well as, an energy device — and why?</li>
</ul>
