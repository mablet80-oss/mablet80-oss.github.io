23:T16c5,<h2 id="what-is-dermal-filler">What is dermal filler?</h2>
<p>Dermal filler is a gel injected under the skin so that it occupies space. That is the whole
mechanism, and holding onto it explains most of what follows: filler works where something is
missing, and it does nothing where the problem is something else.</p>
<p>Most filler used in South Korea is <strong>hyaluronic acid</strong> — a sugar molecule the body already makes, produced
for injection and cross-linked so that it holds its shape and lasts longer than the body's own.
Different products are cross-linked differently, which is why one filler is firm enough to build a
nose bridge and another is soft enough for under the eyes.</p>
<p>An important classification point that changes how you check it: in South Korea, <strong>filler is regulated as
a medical device, not a drug</strong>. Botulinum toxin is a drug. That is why a filler product is looked up
on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's device portal, and a toxin on its drug portal.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-does-and-what-it-does-not">What it does, and what it does not</h2>
<p>Filler adds volume. Placed well, it can restore a hollow that has lost fat, support a fold so it
creases less deeply, or change a contour — a chin, a nose bridge, a cheek.</p>
<p>What it does not do is more useful to know, because it is where most disappointment comes from:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It does not tighten skin.</strong> That is what the ultrasound and radiofrequency devices claim to do.
Adding volume under loose skin fills it; it does not lift it.</li>
<li><strong>It does not relax a muscle.</strong> Lines made by movement — frown lines, crow's feet — are what
botulinum toxin addresses. Filler placed against a moving muscle is fighting the muscle.</li>
<li><strong>It does not improve skin quality.</strong> Texture, pores, and tone are a different set of treatments;
skin boosters and resurfacing lasers sit there.</li>
<li><strong>It is not permanent.</strong> Hyaluronic acid is broken down by the body. That is a feature as much as a
limitation.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-one-thing-worth-understanding-about-reversibility">The one thing worth understanding about reversibility</h2>
<p>Hyaluronic acid filler can be dissolved on purpose. <strong>Hyaluronidase</strong> is an enzyme that breaks it
down, and a doctor can inject it to remove filler that has migrated, over-filled an area, or — far
more urgently — blocked a blood vessel.</p>
<p>This matters when you are choosing. Fillers made of other materials, and semi-permanent products
marketed as longer-lasting, do not have that exit. Asking "what material is this, and can it be
dissolved" is a better question than "how long does it last".</p>
<h2 id="the-serious-risk-stated-plainly">The serious risk, stated plainly</h2>
<p>Filler complications are usually minor: swelling, bruising, lumps that settle or can be massaged.
The complication that matters is <strong>vascular occlusion</strong> — filler entering a blood vessel or pressing
on one hard enough to cut off supply. It is rare. It is also the reason this page has a diagram.</p>
<p>A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of fourteen studies of confirmed occlusion cases found
that the <strong>glabella (between the eyebrows), the nose, and the nasolabial folds</strong> came up most often,
because of the vessels running through them and their connections to the vessels supplying the eye.
Hyaluronic acid accounted for 61.3% of cases, which the authors read as a reflection of how widely
it is used rather than a property of the material — and they note it is the one that can be
reversed.</p>
<p>Outcomes in that review: 58.06% of cases recovered completely, 12.9% were left with permanent
deficits, and <strong>84.2% of hyaluronic acid cases recovered where hyaluronidase was given</strong>. Delay
beyond five days was associated with poor outcomes. The authors' conclusion is about technique,
anatomy, and speed of recognition — not about avoiding filler.</p>
<p>For a patient, that turns into three practical things: know that pain out of proportion, blanching
or mottling of the skin, or any vision change is an emergency and not something to sleep on; ask
whether the clinic keeps hyaluronidase and has used it; and know who you call if it happens after
you fly home.</p>
<h2 id="what-happens-in-a-korean-clinic">What happens in a Korean clinic</h2>
<p>A consultation should establish what is actually being addressed and whether filler is the right
tool for it. Numbing cream is usual, and many products contain lidocaine. The injection itself takes
minutes per area. Swelling and bruising are normal and settle over days; the final shape is what you
see after that, not on the day.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-before-you-agree">Questions to ask before you agree</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which product, and what is its Korean name? Look it up on the device portal.</li>
<li>What material is it, and can it be dissolved?</li>
<li>How much is being used, and in which areas?</li>
<li>Who is injecting — and are they a doctor?</li>
<li>Do you keep hyaluronidase on site?</li>
<li>What do I do, and who do I contact, if something is wrong after I have left South Korea?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Dermal filler adds volume where it is injected and does nothing else — not tightening, not muscle
