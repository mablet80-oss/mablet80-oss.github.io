1d:T140a,<h2 id="what-fractional-means">What "fractional" means</h2>
<p>Not a device — a <strong>way of delivering energy</strong>. Instead of treating the whole surface, the laser
places microscopic columns of injury with untreated skin between them. The intact skin around each
column drives the repair, which is why fractional treatment recovers faster than treating everything
at once.</p>
<p>Within that, two families:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ablative</strong> — CO2 and Er:YAG. Tissue in each column is removed. More recovery.</li>
<li><strong>Non-ablative</strong> — the columns are heated but not removed. Less recovery.</li>
</ul>
<p>In South Korea both are sold under a crowd of platform names. What you need is which family, and at
what settings.</p>
<h2 id="the-finding-that-should-change-how-you-choose">The finding that should change how you choose</h2>
<p>The intuition is that ablative is the serious version and non-ablative is the light one. The pooled
evidence does not support it.</p>
<p>A <strong>meta-analysis of eight studies</strong> compared fractional CO2 with non-CO2 laser therapies for acne
scars and found <strong>no significant difference</strong> on:</p>
<ul>
<li>Clinical improvement</li>
<li><strong>Observer assessment</strong> — p = 0.19</li>
<li><strong>Patient assessment</strong> — p = 0.91</li>
<li><strong>Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation</strong> — p = 0.69</li>
</ul>
<p>The reviewers' summary is that the efficacy of fractional CO2 "appeared to be equal to that of
non-CO2 laser therapies", with a call for better-designed trials.</p>
<p>Two honest caveats. Eight studies is not a large body of work, and the reviewers say so. And "no
significant difference" in a small pooled analysis means a difference was not detected, not that one
cannot exist.</p>
<p>But it does mean the aggressive option is not carrying evidence the gentler one lacks — including on
the pigmentation risk that matters most in Asian skin.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-risk-actually-looks-like-in-asian-skin">What the risk actually looks like in Asian skin</h2>
<p>Numbers rather than warnings.</p>
<p>A <strong>2025 propensity-score-adjusted study of 397 patients</strong> with <strong>Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV</strong>
found post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in <strong>5.42%</strong> treated with fractional laser and <strong>4.71%</strong>
with fractional radiofrequency — no significant difference. Post-treatment scarring was under 1% for
the laser group. No infections.</p>
<p>That is a genuine risk worth a conversation, and it is not the reason to avoid the treatment.</p>
<h2 id="against-rf-microneedling">Against RF microneedling</h2>
<p>The same study is the useful head-to-head, because both are offered for the same problem.</p>
<p>At six months, <strong>88.1%</strong> of the radiofrequency group and <strong>71.9%</strong> of the laser group reached at
least 25% improvement (p = 0.031), with mean improvement of <strong>54.9%</strong> against <strong>47.5%</strong> — a
difference that <strong>did not reach significance</strong> (p = 0.059). Radiofrequency was significantly <strong>more
painful</strong>: 5.65 against 4.14 out of 10.</p>
<p>The authors conclude that <strong>both are effective and well tolerated</strong> in this population. So this is a
choice between two workable options, not a right and a wrong one.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-actually-decide-it">What should actually decide it</h2>
<p>Since the outcome data do not separate the options, decide on the things that do differ:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Downtime.</strong> Ablative needs more. If you are here for eight days, that is a real constraint.</li>
<li><strong>Pain.</strong> Higher with radiofrequency in the head-to-head; higher with ablative among lasers.</li>
<li><strong>Your skin type and history.</strong> If you pigment easily or have pigmented after a previous
procedure, say so before anything else.</li>
<li><strong>The operator.</strong> Across every evidence base on this site, settings and experience move outcomes
more than the brand does.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-at-a-consultation">What to ask at a consultation</h2>
<ul>
<li>Ablative or non-ablative, and <strong>why that one for me</strong>?</li>
<li>What <strong>settings</strong>, and what recovery should I expect in days?</li>
<li>What is the <strong>plan if I hyperpigment</strong> afterwards — and can you treat it before I fly home?</li>
<li>How many sessions, at what interval, and what is the total?</li>
<li>What is the device's <strong>Korean product name</strong>?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Fractional lasers work in columns, and the pooled trials found fractional CO2 and non-ablative
lasers equal on clinician assessment, patient assessment and pigmentation risk — so the more
aggressive option is not buying a better result in the data. In Fitzpatrick III–IV skin,
post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation ran around 5% for both fractional laser and fractional
radiofrequency. Choose on downtime, pain, and the operator, and ask what happens if you pigment
