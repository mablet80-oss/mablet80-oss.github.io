Juvelook and PDLLA: a collagen stimulator, not a filler

PDLLA works by provoking your own collagen, so it is slow and not reversible the way hyaluronic acid is. A 2026 split-face trial found it non-inferior to PLLA.

Short answer

What is Juvelook and how is PDLLA different from filler?

Juvelook is built on poly-D,L-lactic acid, a collagen stimulator: it does not fill space itself but provokes your own collagen over months. That makes it slow to show and — unlike hyaluronic acid filler — not straightforwardly reversible. A 2026 split-face trial in 33 people found a PDLLA-based product non-inferior to PLLA for nasolabial folds, with both significantly improving wrinkle severity.

What a collagen stimulator is

Hyaluronic acid filler works by being there. Inject it, and the gel occupies space; the change is immediate and it lasts as long as the gel does.

A collagen stimulator works differently. The injected material is not the point — the body's reaction to it is. Poly-D,L-lactic acid (PDLLA) is a biodegradable polymer that provokes fibroblasts to lay down new collagen over the following months, after which the polymer itself is broken down and gone. What remains, if it works, is your own tissue.

Juvelook is built on PDLLA, in a formulation that also contains non-crosslinked hyaluronic acid.

Three consequences of that mechanism

Everything a patient needs to decide follows from the mechanism, and none of it is obvious from the marketing.

It is slow. Collagen is laid down over months, not days. The trial below measured to week 24. Judging this treatment at two weeks tells you nothing.

Early volume is not the result. Because these products often include hyaluronic acid, there can be a little immediate fullness that then settles as the HA disperses. Mistaking that for the outcome leads people to conclude too early, in either direction.

It cannot simply be dissolved. Hyaluronidase breaks down hyaluronic acid. It does not remove PDLLA. This is the single most important practical difference from HA filler, and it argues for placing less rather than more, particularly the first time.

What the trial found

A 2026 split-face study in Skin Research and Technology compared a PDLLA-based product with PLLA — the class Sculptra belongs to — in 33 subjects, mean age 48.5.

The design is its strength: each person received PDLLA on one nasolabial fold and PLLA on the other, so each participant acted as their own control. Three injections at weeks 0, 4, and 8; assessment out to week 24.

  • Both products significantly reduced wrinkle severity at every timepoint against baseline (p < 0.001).
  • PDLLA improved wrinkle severity by −1.37 at week 24, PLLA by −1.20 — with no statistically significant difference between them on non-inferiority testing.
  • 80% of patients were satisfied or greatly satisfied; 70% had no preference between the two sides of their own face.

That last figure is the most informative in the study. When people cannot tell their own two sides apart, claims that one polymer is meaningfully superior deserve scepticism.

The authors' conclusion is appropriately narrow: "The PDLLA based product demonstrated non-inferiority to PLLA for NLF correction. Further studies are needed to assess long-term safety."

The limits of that evidence

Thirty-three people. One anatomical area. Twenty-four weeks. A well-designed study, but a small one, and the authors themselves flag long-term safety as unsettled.

Collagen stimulators as a class have a known late complication in nodule formation, and "long-term safety" is the phrase doing that work in the conclusion. It is a reason to prefer an experienced injector and a conservative first treatment, not a reason to avoid the class.

Side effects reported

Most subjects had mild swelling for three to four days. One had moderate swelling and tenderness after a second PDLLA injection, which resolved with an intralesional steroid and hyaluronidase.

Worth reading that carefully: the hyaluronidase was acting on the hyaluronic acid component of the product. It does not dissolve the PDLLA.

Questions to ask a Korean clinic

  • Which product exactly, and what is its Korean name? Check its licence.
  • Is it PDLLA, PLLA, or something else — and does it contain hyaluronic acid?
  • How many sessions, at what interval, and what is the total?
  • When should I judge the result?
  • What is the plan if it is uneven, given it cannot simply be dissolved?

The bottom line

Juvelook is a PDLLA collagen stimulator: it provokes your own collagen over months rather than filling space on the day, and it cannot be dissolved the way hyaluronic acid can. A 2026 split-face trial in 33 people found it non-inferior to PLLA for nasolabial folds, with both significantly improving wrinkle severity and 70% of patients unable to prefer one side over the other. The evidence is good in design and small in size, and the authors call long-term safety unsettled.

Frequently asked questions

Is Juvelook a filler?
Not in the way hyaluronic acid filler is. HA filler occupies space itself, immediately. A collagen stimulator provokes your body to make collagen, and the change appears over months. Products in this class often include some non-crosslinked hyaluronic acid, which can give a little early volume that then settles — so what you see at two weeks is not the result.
Can it be dissolved if I do not like it?
Not the way hyaluronic acid can. Hyaluronidase breaks down HA; it does not remove poly-D,L-lactic acid. That is the single most important practical difference between this class and HA filler, and it is a reason to be more conservative about how much is placed and where.
What is the difference between PDLLA and PLLA?
Both are lactic acid polymers used as collagen stimulators; Sculptra is the best-known PLLA. In a 2026 split-face trial of 33 people, a PDLLA-based product was non-inferior to PLLA for nasolabial folds — mean wrinkle severity improvement of −1.37 against −1.20 at week 24, with no statistically significant difference. 70% of patients had no preference between the two sides.
How strong is the evidence?
The split-face trial is a good design — each person acts as their own control — but it is 33 people and one anatomical area, and the authors say directly that further studies are needed to assess long-term safety.
What are the side effects?
Mild swelling for three to four days in most subjects. One participant had moderate swelling and tenderness after a second injection, treated with intralesional steroid and hyaluronidase — the hyaluronidase acting on the hyaluronic acid component of the product, not on the PDLLA.
What should I ask a clinic?
Exactly which product and its Korean name. Whether it is PDLLA, PLLA, or something else, and whether it contains hyaluronic acid. How many sessions and the total. And what the plan is if the result is uneven — given it cannot simply be dissolved.
