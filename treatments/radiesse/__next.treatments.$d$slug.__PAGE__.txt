1c:T16b4,<h2 id="what-it-is">What it is</h2>
<p><strong>Calcium hydroxylapatite microspheres</strong> suspended in a <strong>carboxymethylcellulose gel</strong>. The gel
gives correction on the day. The microspheres stay behind and provoke a collagen response over the
following months, then break down.</p>
<p>That dual action is the selling point, and it is real. It also means Radiesse sits awkwardly between
two categories — part filler, part collagen stimulator — and the two versions sold in Seoul are
almost different treatments.</p>
<h2 id="the-licensed-version-and-the-evidence-for-it">The licensed version, and the evidence for it</h2>
<p>A <strong>2024 systematic review</strong> in the <em>International Journal of Dermatology</em> assessed CaHA/CMC across
facial uses and found it <em>"may be considered a safe and effective treatment option for cheeks,
jawline, HIV-related facial lipoatrophy, and nasolabial folds"</em>, with marionette lines, chin,
pre-jowl and corner of the mouth also responding well.</p>
<p>On the body, the companion review notes the only <strong>FDA-approved body indication is the hands</strong>, with
a European approval for the <strong>décolletage</strong>.</p>
<p>That is a reasonable evidence base for a contour product.</p>
<h2 id="the-hyperdiluted-version-and-the-evidence-for-it">The hyperdiluted version, and the evidence for it</h2>
<p>Here is the gap worth knowing before a consultation in Seoul.</p>
<p><strong>Hyperdilute Radiesse</strong> — the same product reconstituted with much more diluent and spread thinly —
is marketed everywhere for skin quality, tightening, neck, décolletage, arms, thighs and abdomen.</p>
<p>Both 2024 reviews land on the same sentence. The face review: <em>"Despite the recent trend,
guidelines, and safety profile of diluted and hyperdiluted Radiesse, no randomized controlled trials
have been published."</em> The body review: hyperdilutions on neck, feet, arms, thighs and abdomen "have
yet to be supported by randomized controlled trials".</p>
<p>Note the tension inside that second abstract, because it is characteristic of this whole area: the
authors describe a skin-tightening effect as established in practice <strong>and</strong> say randomised trials
do not support it. Both statements are in the same paragraph. What that means for you is that the
claim rests on consensus and clinical experience, not on trial data — which is a legitimate basis
for a treatment, and not a basis for a percentage figure.</p>
<p>If a clinic quotes you a specific improvement number for hyperdilute Radiesse, ask where it comes
from.</p>
<h2 id="nothing-dissolves-it">Nothing dissolves it</h2>
<p>This is the practical difference from hyaluronic acid filler, and it should weigh more than either
evidence base.</p>
<p>A <strong>2026 review</strong> of filler complications puts it plainly: <em>"CaHA is not dissolvable with any
readily available enzyme such as hyaluronidase for HA fillers."</em> In vascular occlusion — the
emergency that makes reversibility matter — <em>"no enzymatic agent can dissolve CaHA microspheres"</em>,
although some consensus guidelines still describe hyaluronidase in the area, to address any
hyaluronic acid also present or to improve tissue permeability.</p>
<p>So the hyaluronic acid reassurance does not transfer. A result you dislike is waited out.</p>
<h2 id="how-it-compares-with-hyaluronic-acid-filler">How it compares with hyaluronic acid filler</h2>
<p>In <strong>ordinary use</strong>, the complication profiles are close. A meta-analysis of <strong>four randomised
nasolabial trials</strong> found no significant difference between CaHA and hyaluronic acid for <strong>nodules</strong>
(RR 0.18; 95% CI 0.01–6.62) or <strong>haematoma</strong> (RR 0.24; 95% CI 0.01–4.31). Those confidence intervals
are very wide, so read it as "no difference detected in small trials" rather than as proof of
equivalence.</p>

<h2 id="what-to-ask-at-a-consultation">What to ask at a consultation</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Standard concentration or hyperdiluted?</strong> These are different treatments with different
evidence, and the answer should be volunteered.</li>
<li><strong>Which site, and is it on-label?</strong> Hands is the approved body site; neck, arms and abdomen are
not.</li>
<li><strong>What happens if there is a problem?</strong> Nothing dissolves it, so the answer should be a real plan.</li>
<li><strong>What exactly are you expecting to change, and when?</strong></li>
<li><strong>Korean product name</strong>, so you can check the device licence yourself.</li>
</ul>

<p>Radiesse corrects immediately and stimulates collagen afterwards, and for cheeks, jawline and
nasolabial folds a 2024 systematic review supports it as safe and effective. The hyperdiluted
version sold for skin quality and body tightening is supported by consensus and practice, not by
randomised trials — both 2024 reviews say so explicitly. And unlike hyaluronic acid filler, no
enzyme dissolves it, which should make a first treatment conservative and the choice of injector
