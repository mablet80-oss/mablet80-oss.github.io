Potenza and microneedle radiofrequency: what it treats well

Needles carry radiofrequency past the epidermis. A 2026 review of 41 studies found credible efficacy for acne scars and safety across skin types.

Short answer

What does Potenza do, and what is microneedle radiofrequency good for?

Potenza is a microneedle radiofrequency device: fine needles carry the energy through the epidermis and release it at a depth the operator sets. A 2026 systematic review of 41 studies found credible efficacy across several indications — 26–100% reduction in acne scar scores, comparable to fractional lasers — with mild, transient side effects and consistent safety across Fitzpatrick skin types, which is the property that distinguishes it from many laser options.

Key facts

Technology
Fractional microneedle radiofrequency — insulated or non-insulated needles delivering energy at a chosen depth
Purpose
Atrophic acne scars, skin laxity, photoaging, pores, and several other dermatological indications
Pain
Topical anaesthetic is standard; the needling and the heat are both felt
Downtime
Redness, swelling, and pinpoint marks; the review describes an attractive recovery profile
Sessions
A course, typically several sessions at intervals
Results timeline
Builds over the course and the months after it, as remodelling continues

What microneedle radiofrequency is

Ordinary radiofrequency devices put energy in through the skin surface, which means the epidermis takes heat on the way to wherever the treatment is aimed. Microneedle radiofrequency avoids that: fine needles carry the current through the epidermis and release it at a depth the operator sets.

So there are two mechanisms working at once — the mechanical injury of the needle and the thermal injury of the energy at its tip — and one important property: the surface is comparatively spared.

Potenza is one such device. Sylfirm is another. Korean clinics sell them by name rather than by category, which is why the three arrangements are worth knowing.

What the evidence supports

A 2026 systematic review in Aesthetic Plastic Surgery covered 41 studies — 15 randomised controlled trials, 19 prospective non-randomised studies, 5 retrospective cohorts, 2 case series — totalling over a thousand patients across several countries. What it found, by indication:

Indication Reported improvement
Atrophic acne scars 26–100% reduction in scar scores; comparable to fractional lasers
Skin laxity and wrinkles 18–75%
Photoaging 25–100% wrinkle improvement
Rosacea 81% of patients improved
Stretch marks Comparable to CO2 laser
Underarm sweating Botulinum toxin outperformed it

Those ranges are wide, and the width is the honest part: different scar types, devices, needle depths, and energy settings produce genuinely different results. The reviewers' conclusion asks for "rigorous reporting of device settings" precisely because that variation is not yet controlled for.

The property that makes it a good fit here

For an international audience — and this site's readers are largely in East and Southeast Asia and Australia — the finding that matters most is about skin type.

The review reports consistent safety across Fitzpatrick skin types, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in 2.9–5% of cases, mild and transient adverse events, and no serious complications across the included trials.

That is a meaningful contrast with ablative and high-fluence laser treatment, where the central risk in pigmented skin is provoking more pigment. Because the energy is delivered past the epidermis rather than through it, microneedle radiofrequency sidesteps much of that. It is a large part of why it is chosen for Asian skin, and it is a better reason than the brand marketing usually given.

Acne scars specifically

This is where the evidence is strongest and where the treatment is most worth considering. Scar scores fell by 26–100% across the studies, and the reviewers describe efficacy as comparable to fractional lasers.

What that does not mean is a single-session fix. Atrophic scars respond over a course and continue remodelling for months afterwards, and the type of scar matters — different scar shapes respond at different rates. A clinic that assesses your scar types before proposing a number of sessions is doing the right thing; one that quotes a package before looking is not.

Brand against brand

Potenza and Sylfirm are both sold with claims of distinct advantages. The systematic review's closing request — standardised outcomes, longer follow-up, and rigorous reporting of device settings to "facilitate head-to-head device comparisons" — tells you those comparisons have not been done.

So a claim that one device outperforms another is, at present, unevidenced. What is evidenced is the category.

Questions to ask a Korean clinic

  • Which device, and what is its Korean product name?
  • What needle depth and energy level, and why those for my skin?
  • Are the needles insulated, and what difference does that make here?
  • What scar types do I have, and how does that change the plan?
  • How many sessions, at what interval, and what is the total?

The bottom line

Microneedle radiofrequency delivers energy past the epidermis to a depth the operator chooses. A 2026 review of 41 studies found credible efficacy across several indications — strongest for atrophic acne scars, where it is comparable to fractional lasers — with mild, transient side effects and consistent safety across skin types, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation at 2.9–5%. That skin type safety is the real argument for it. Claims that one brand beats another are not yet supported by evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Does it work for acne scars?
This is its strongest indication in the published evidence. The 2026 review of 41 studies reports 26–100% reduction in acne scar scores and describes efficacy as comparable to fractional lasers. That is a wide range, reflecting different scar types, devices, and protocols.
Is it safe for darker skin?
This is the practical reason it is often chosen. The review reports consistent safety across Fitzpatrick skin types, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in 2.9–5% of cases and no serious complications in the included trials. Because the needles carry the energy past the epidermis, surface pigment is comparatively spared — the opposite of the problem that makes ablative laser risky in pigmented skin.
How does it compare with a fractional laser?
For acne scars the review describes the two as comparable. The difference is in the risk profile rather than the result, which is why microneedle radiofrequency is frequently preferred in skin that pigments easily.
What else is it used for?
The review covers atrophic acne scars, skin laxity, photoaging, periorbital wrinkles, rosacea, melasma, stretch marks, underarm sweating, and acne. Improvements were 18–75% for laxity and wrinkles and 25–100% for photoaging, and 81% of rosacea patients improved. For underarm sweating, botulinum toxin outperformed it.
Is Potenza different from Sylfirm?
They are different microneedle radiofrequency devices. The review's own conclusion calls for rigorous reporting of device settings to allow head-to-head comparisons — which is a polite way of saying those comparisons do not yet exist. Treat brand-versus-brand claims as unevidenced.
What should I ask a clinic?
Needle depth, energy level, number of passes, and why those for your skin. Whether the needles are insulated. How many sessions and the total for the course. And who is operating it.
