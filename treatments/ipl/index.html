IPL in South Korea: what intense pulsed light treats and what to expect

IPL is a flash-lamp light treatment for sun spots and redness. How it differs from a laser, what it treats in Asian skin, sessions, and what to ask.

Short answer

What is IPL and is it worth doing in Korea?

IPL, or intense pulsed light, is a flash-lamp treatment that sends a broad band of filtered light into the skin, rather than the single wavelength of a laser. It is used mainly for sun spots, freckles and facial redness, and studies in Asian patients report good improvement in both. A course is usually several sessions three to six weeks apart, with brown spots darkening and flaking off over about a week. In South Korea it is offered in skin clinics, often under device names such as M22 or BBL.

What is IPL?

IPL stands for intense pulsed light. It is a flash-lamp device that sends short pulses of bright light into the skin to treat brown spots and redness.

It is often called a photofacial. In South Korea you will also see it listed under device names, such as M22 or BBL.

How does IPL work?

A laser emits light of one wavelength. IPL emits a broad spectrum of light in each pulse, and filters narrow it to the range that suits the target.

The light is absorbed by pigment in a brown spot or by blood in a small vessel. The heat breaks up the pigment or closes the vessel, while the surrounding skin is largely spared. A 2010 review found that most comparative trials rate IPL as broadly similar in effectiveness to lasers.

What is IPL used for?

IPL is used mainly for:

  • Sun spots and freckles. In Japanese patients, small sun spots responded well; large, thick patches responded less.
  • Facial redness and small visible vessels. The European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology names lasers and IPL as the treatment of choice for small facial vessels, and the AAD notes IPL can reduce rosacea flushing.
  • Overall tone in sun-damaged skin. In a study of 97 Asian patients, over 90% were rated good or excellent for pigmentation and over 83% for small vessels.

It is less effective for wrinkles. A split-face study in Chinese women found IPL improved spots, vessels and redness but did less for wrinkles.

Who is IPL suitable for?

IPL suits people whose main concern is uneven colour: sun spots, freckles, redness, or a mix. Much of the published evidence comes from Asian patients, including Japanese and Chinese.

A review of laser treatment in Asian skin found IPL carries less risk of post-inflammatory darkening than Q-switched lasers, at the cost of needing more sessions.

Who should avoid IPL?

Avoid IPL with a tan or recent sun exposure. Baylor College of Medicine notes that a tan interferes with the treatment, and DermNet advises avoiding sun in the days and weeks before and after.

Melasma needs a doctor's assessment first. The evidence is mixed: studies show improvement, mostly when IPL is combined with other treatments, but a 2023 network meta-analysis found IPL had the highest rate of side effects among melasma treatments, and it can flare melasma in some people. For melasma, our pigmentation page compares the options, including laser toning.

Tell the doctor about any medicines you take and any history of darkening after skin injury.

IPL devices and brand names in Korea

Korean clinics usually list IPL by the device name. Common names include:

  • M22 (Lumenis), cleared by the US FDA for pigmented and vascular lesions, rosacea redness and mild to moderate acne.
  • BBL (Sciton), short for BroadBand Light. Sciton describes it alongside "other IPL devices", so for a patient it belongs to the same family.

Clinics may also call it 포토페이셜 (photofacial) or 아이피엘. Ask which device is being used. Device clearance in Korea can be checked on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's medical device portal.

Who performs IPL in Korea?

Korean law limits skin-care beauty businesses to treatments without medical devices or medicines. IPL devices are medical devices, so in South Korea IPL is offered in medical clinics rather than beauty salons.

That difference matters. An Australian case series described IPL injuries in skin of colour at cosmetic clinics, linked to broad wavelengths and operator skill. A clinic that treats Asian skin every day is a sensible place to have it.

What happens during an IPL session?

The skin is cleansed, a cooling gel may be applied, and eyes are protected. The handpiece is pressed against the skin and each pulse gives a bright flash.

How painful is IPL?

Most people describe a snap or a quick warm sensation with each pulse. Redness for one to three days is usual, and there may be a little bruising.

IPL downtime and results timeline

There is little downtime. Brown spots typically darken and flake off over about 5 to 7 days. A Japanese study described tiny crusts carrying pigment forming over sun spots and dropping off, which is how the spot clears.

For small vessels, the EADV says swelling and redness fade in four to six days and the vessels disappear over 10 to 20 days. Use SPF 50+ daily afterwards.

Side effects and risks

Most side effects are short-lived: redness, swelling, and occasional bruising. DermNet notes that blistering can occur rarely, and that damage to pigment cells can leave darker or paler patches.

The EADV notes that the wrong choice of device or settings can cause temporary darkening or longer-lasting lightening. The risk is higher with a tan, and lower with conservative settings in skilled hands.

How many sessions are usually recommended?

DermNet and Singapore General Hospital both describe a course of four to six sessions, three to six weeks apart. For small facial vessels, one or two sessions are often enough.

On a short trip, one session can lighten spots before you fly home. The darkening and flaking phase lasts about a week, so allow for it in your plans. For a full course, plan repeat visits or continue at home.

What should foreigners ask a Korean clinic?

  • Which device will be used, and why it suits my skin?
  • What is it treating: spots, redness, or both?
  • Is my pigmentation melasma? If so, is IPL the right choice?
  • How many sessions do you recommend, and what can one session achieve?
  • What should I expect in the week after, given my flight date?

See flying after treatment for timing around a flight and more than one treatment if IPL is part of a combined plan.

The bottom line

IPL is a broad-spectrum flash-lamp treatment, not a laser, used mainly for sun spots, freckles and facial redness. Studies in Asian patients report good results for both, typically over four to six sessions a few weeks apart, with spots darkening and flaking off over about a week. In South Korea it is offered in medical clinics under device names such as M22 and BBL; have melasma assessed by a doctor before choosing it.

Frequently asked questions

Is IPL the same as a laser?
No. A laser emits one wavelength; IPL emits a broad spectrum of light in each pulse, narrowed with filters. Both work by heating a target such as pigment or blood vessels. A review of comparative trials found IPL broadly similar in effectiveness to lasers for many uses.
What does IPL treat best?
Small sun spots, freckles and facial redness. In a study of 97 Asian patients, over 90% were rated good or excellent for pigmentation and over 83% for small vessels. It is less effective for wrinkles.
How many sessions will I need?
Usually four to six, every three to six weeks, according to DermNet and Singapore General Hospital. For small facial vessels, the EADV says one or two sessions are usually needed.
Is IPL safe for Asian skin?
It is widely used in Asian skin, and a review of laser treatment in Asian patients found IPL carries less risk of post-inflammatory darkening than Q-switched lasers, though it needs more sessions. The main risks are temporary darkening or lightening of the skin, more likely with a tan or the wrong settings.
Does IPL help melasma?
The evidence is mixed. Studies show improvement, mostly when IPL is combined with other treatments, but one analysis found IPL had the highest rate of side effects among melasma treatments, and it can flare melasma in some people. Melasma needs a doctor's assessment first.
Is BBL the same as IPL?
BBL (BroadBand Light) is Sciton's brand of broad-spectrum light device, and Sciton compares it with "other IPL devices". For a patient it belongs to the same family of treatment.

Medical evidence

  1. Systematic review2010
    Intense pulsed light (IPL): a review

    Most comparative trials attest IPL similar effectiveness to lasers, at a level of evidence of 2b to 4.

  2. Systematic review2014
    Application of intense pulsed light in the treatment of dermatologic disease: a systematic review

    Level 1 evidence was found for melasma, acne vulgaris and telangiectasia, and level 2 evidence for lentiginous disease and rosacea.

  3. Case series2001n = 97
    Photorejuvenation of Asian skin with intense pulsed light

    More than 90% of patients rated good or excellent for pigmentation, more than 83% for telangiectasia and more than 65% for texture, with three minor blisters leaving no marks.

  4. Systematic review2023n = 1182
    Comparative efficacy and safety of treatments for melasma: a network meta-analysis

    The incidence rate of adverse reactions was highest when intense pulsed light was used.
