26:T1cc7,<h2 id="what-is-ipl">What is IPL?</h2>
<p>IPL stands for intense pulsed light. It is a flash-lamp device that sends short pulses of bright light into the skin to treat brown spots and redness.</p>
<p>It is often called a photofacial. In South Korea you will also see it listed under device names, such as M22 or BBL.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-ipl-work">How does IPL work?</h2>
<p>A laser emits light of one wavelength. IPL emits a <strong>broad spectrum</strong> of light in each pulse, and filters narrow it to the range that suits the target.</p>
<p>The light is absorbed by pigment in a brown spot or by blood in a small vessel. The heat breaks up the pigment or closes the vessel, while the surrounding skin is largely spared. A 2010 review found that most comparative trials rate IPL as broadly similar in effectiveness to lasers.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-ipl-used-for">What is IPL used for?</h2>
<p>IPL is used mainly for:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sun spots and freckles.</strong> In Japanese patients, small sun spots responded well; large, thick patches responded less.</li>
<li><strong>Facial redness and small visible vessels.</strong> The European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology names lasers and IPL as the treatment of choice for small facial vessels, and the AAD notes IPL can reduce rosacea flushing.</li>
<li><strong>Overall tone in sun-damaged skin.</strong> In a study of 97 Asian patients, over 90% were rated good or excellent for pigmentation and over 83% for small vessels.</li>
</ul>
<p>It is less effective for wrinkles. A split-face study in Chinese women found IPL improved spots, vessels and redness but did less for wrinkles.</p>
<h2 id="who-is-ipl-suitable-for">Who is IPL suitable for?</h2>
<p>IPL suits people whose main concern is uneven colour: sun spots, freckles, redness, or a mix. Much of the published evidence comes from Asian patients, including Japanese and Chinese.</p>
<p>A review of laser treatment in Asian skin found IPL carries <strong>less risk of post-inflammatory darkening</strong> than Q-switched lasers, at the cost of needing more sessions.</p>
<h2 id="who-should-avoid-ipl">Who should avoid IPL?</h2>
<p>Avoid IPL with a tan or recent sun exposure. Baylor College of Medicine notes that a tan interferes with the treatment, and DermNet advises avoiding sun in the days and weeks before and after.</p>
<p><strong>Melasma needs a doctor's assessment first.</strong> The evidence is mixed: studies show improvement, mostly when IPL is combined with other treatments, but a 2023 network meta-analysis found IPL had the highest rate of side effects among melasma treatments, and it can flare melasma in some people. For melasma, our <a href="/concerns/pigmentation/">pigmentation</a> page compares the options, including <a href="/treatments/laser-toning/">laser toning</a>.</p>
<p>Tell the doctor about any medicines you take and any history of darkening after skin injury.</p>
<h2 id="ipl-devices-and-brand-names-in-korea">IPL devices and brand names in Korea</h2>
<p>Korean clinics usually list IPL by the device name. Common names include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>M22</strong> (Lumenis), cleared by the US FDA for pigmented and vascular lesions, rosacea redness and mild to moderate acne.</li>
<li><strong>BBL</strong> (Sciton), short for BroadBand Light. Sciton describes it alongside "other IPL devices", so for a patient it belongs to the same family.</li>
</ul>
<p>Clinics may also call it 포토페이셜 (photofacial) or 아이피엘. Ask which device is being used. Device clearance in Korea can be checked on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's <a href="https://emedi.mfds.go.kr/">medical device portal</a>.</p>
<h2 id="who-performs-ipl-in-korea">Who performs IPL in Korea?</h2>
<p>Korean law limits skin-care beauty businesses to treatments <strong>without medical devices or medicines</strong>. IPL devices are medical devices, so in South Korea IPL is offered in medical clinics rather than beauty salons.</p>
<p>That difference matters. An Australian case series described IPL injuries in skin of colour at cosmetic clinics, linked to broad wavelengths and operator skill. A clinic that treats Asian skin every day is a sensible place to have it.</p>
<h2 id="what-happens-during-an-ipl-session">What happens during an IPL session?</h2>
<p>The skin is cleansed, a cooling gel may be applied, and eyes are protected. The handpiece is pressed against the skin and each pulse gives a bright flash.</p>
<h2 id="how-painful-is-ipl">How painful is IPL?</h2>
<p>Most people describe a snap or a quick warm sensation with each pulse. Redness for one to three days is usual, and there may be a little bruising.</p>
<h2 id="ipl-downtime-and-results-timeline">IPL downtime and results timeline</h2>
<p>There is little downtime. Brown spots typically <strong>darken and flake off over about 5 to 7 days</strong>. A Japanese study described tiny crusts carrying pigment forming over sun spots and dropping off, which is how the spot clears.</p>
<p>For small vessels, the EADV says swelling and redness fade in four to six days and the vessels disappear over 10 to 20 days. Use SPF 50+ daily afterwards.</p>
<h2 id="side-effects-and-risks">Side effects and risks</h2>
<p>Most side effects are short-lived: redness, swelling, and occasional bruising. DermNet notes that blistering can occur rarely, and that damage to pigment cells can leave darker or paler patches.</p>
<p>The EADV notes that the wrong choice of device or settings can cause temporary darkening or longer-lasting lightening. The risk is higher with a tan, and lower with conservative settings in skilled hands.</p>
<h2 id="how-many-sessions-are-usually-recommended">How many sessions are usually recommended?</h2>
<p>DermNet and Singapore General Hospital both describe a course of <strong>four to six sessions</strong>, three to six weeks apart. For small facial vessels, one or two sessions are often enough.</p>
<p>On a short trip, one session can lighten spots before you fly home. The darkening and flaking phase lasts about a week, so allow for it in your plans. For a full course, plan repeat visits or continue at home.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-foreigners-ask-a-korean-clinic">What should foreigners ask a Korean clinic?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which device</strong> will be used, and why it suits my skin?</li>
<li><strong>What is it treating</strong>: spots, redness, or both?</li>
<li><strong>Is my pigmentation melasma?</strong> If so, is IPL the right choice?</li>
<li><strong>How many sessions</strong> do you recommend, and what can one session achieve?</li>
<li><strong>What should I expect</strong> in the week after, given my flight date?</li>
</ul>
<p>See <a href="/visit/flying-after-treatment/">flying after treatment</a> for timing around a flight and <a href="/visit/more-than-one-treatment/">more than one treatment</a> if IPL is part of a combined plan.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>IPL is a broad-spectrum flash-lamp treatment, not a laser, used mainly for sun spots, freckles and facial redness. Studies in Asian patients report good results for both, typically over four to six sessions a few weeks apart, with spots darkening and flaking off over about a week.
