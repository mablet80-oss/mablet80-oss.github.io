23:T11c0,<h2 id="inmode-is-not-a-treatment">"InMode" is not a treatment</h2>
<p>A clinic offering you "InMode" has not told you what they intend to do. InMode is a <strong>platform</strong> —
a console with several different handpieces attached to it — and the handpieces do genuinely
different things, with different levels of invasiveness.</p>
<p>The three you are most likely to meet:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Forma</strong> — bipolar radiofrequency applied <strong>on the skin surface</strong>. Non-invasive, gentle, a course.</li>
<li><strong>Morpheus8</strong> — <strong>fractional radiofrequency microneedling</strong>. Needles carry the energy past the
epidermis to a set depth.</li>
<li><strong>FaceTite</strong> — radiofrequency delivered <strong>under the skin</strong> through a cannula.</li>
</ul>
<p>That third one is not a variation on the first two. It is a different category of procedure, and
being clear about which you are being sold is the entire point of this page.</p>
<h2 id="bipolar-and-what-it-buys">Bipolar, and what it buys</h2>
<p>Bipolar radiofrequency passes current between two electrodes rather than through the body to a
grounding pad. The 2025 review gives it a penetration depth of <strong>1–4 mm</strong>, against up to <strong>20 mm</strong>
for monopolar.</p>
<p>Shallower, but with <strong>more controlled and localised energy distribution and less discomfort</strong> — the
trade-off stated plainly. Depth is not automatically better; it depends what you are trying to reach.</p>
<h2 id="the-distinction-that-matters-on-the-skin-or-under-it">The distinction that matters: on the skin, or under it</h2>
<p>Non-invasive radiofrequency puts energy through the surface. Microneedling puts it through needles.
<strong>Subdermal radiofrequency puts a cannula under your skin.</strong></p>
<p>The 2020 review in <em>Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery — Global Open</em> lists what has been reported in
the minimally invasive category: erythema, <strong>prolonged swelling past two months</strong>, <strong>subdermal
banding</strong>, and potential burns, blistering, and transient hyperpigmentation.</p>
<p>None of that is catastrophic, and the technique exists precisely because it does more than surface
treatment can. But it is a different consent conversation from a facial. If a clinic describes
FaceTite in the same breath and the same tone as a Forma session, that is the moment to slow down
and ask about anaesthesia, recovery, and who is holding the cannula.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-outcome-data-shows">What the outcome data shows</h2>
<p>The largest published evaluation of this combination — bipolar radiofrequency together with
fractional radiofrequency microneedling — was a retrospective multicentre series of <strong>247 patients</strong>.
It reported a mean <strong>Baker Face Neck Classification improvement of 1.4</strong> and a <strong>93% satisfaction
rate</strong>.</p>
<p>A separate bipolar radiofrequency study reported <strong>over 90% satisfaction at six months</strong>, with two
minor complications: one burn and one seroma.</p>
<p>Two honest caveats. The 247-patient series is <strong>retrospective</strong>, which is weaker evidence than a
randomised trial. And both figures are for combinations and specific protocols, not for "InMode" as
a name.</p>
<p>The reviewers' framing is the useful one: radiofrequency <em>"bridges an important treatment gap"</em> for
people who will not have surgery but need more than a non-invasive device offers. That is a claim
about a gap, not a claim to fill the role of surgery.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-a-korean-clinic">Questions to ask a Korean clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which handpiece?</strong> Forma, Morpheus8, FaceTite, or something else.</li>
<li>Is the energy applied on the skin, through needles, or under the skin?</li>
<li>If under the skin: what anaesthesia, what recovery, and who performs it?</li>
<li>What is the device's Korean product name, so I can check the licence?</li>
<li>How many sessions, and what is the total?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>InMode is a platform, and the handpiece is the treatment. Bipolar radiofrequency reaches 1–4 mm with
more controlled energy and less discomfort than monopolar. Reported outcomes for the combination
approach are good — a 1.4 Baker improvement and 93% satisfaction in 247 patients — but the series is
retrospective, and one of the handpieces goes under your skin. Ask which one you are being offered
