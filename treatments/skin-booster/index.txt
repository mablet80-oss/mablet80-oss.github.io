1d:T1483,<h2 id="skin-booster-is-a-category-not-a-product">"Skin booster" is a category, not a product</h2>
<p>This is the first thing to understand, because the term is doing a lot of marketing work. A skin
booster is not a specific substance. It is a <strong>way of injecting</strong>: many shallow deposits spread
across an area, intended to change the quality of the skin rather than its shape.</p>
<p>What is in the syringe varies. Most often it is hyaluronic acid in a thin, uncross-linked or lightly
cross-linked form. It may instead be polynucleotides, or a mixture including vitamins and other
actives. These products have different evidence bases and are not interchangeable, but they are all
sold under the same two English words.</p>
<p>So the useful question is never "should I get a skin booster". It is "what exactly are you
injecting, and what is the evidence for that".</p>
<h2 id="how-it-differs-from-filler">How it differs from filler</h2>
<p>Both are often hyaluronic acid, which is why the confusion persists.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Filler</strong> goes in one place, deeper, in a cohesive gel form, to occupy space and change a contour.</li>
<li><strong>A booster</strong> is spread thinly and shallowly over an area, to change how the skin behaves —
hydration, elasticity, fine surface lines.</li>
</ul>
<p>Same material, entirely different intent. A booster will not build a cheek, and a filler will not
improve skin texture across a face.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-controlled-evidence-shows">What the controlled evidence shows</h2>
<p>A <strong>2026 multicentre randomised controlled study</strong> in <em>Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery — Global
Open</em> tested a sodium hyaluronate composite solution given by mesotherapy in <strong>439 participants</strong>
(287 treated, 152 control). The measurable findings:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Skin hydration</strong> rose by an average of <strong>26.50 units</strong> at peak, with a <strong>17.35-point</strong> gain still
present at three months.</li>
<li><strong>Skin elasticity</strong> rose by an average of <strong>18.18 units</strong> at one month, with <strong>11.06 points</strong>
sustained at three months.</li>
<li><strong>Adverse events in 1.67%</strong> of cases — four of mild to moderate hyperpigmentation and one moderate
allergic reaction. None serious.</li>
</ul>
<p>Those are real, measured, controlled results, and they are the strongest evidence on this page.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-read-the-97-figure">How to read the 97% figure</h2>
<p>The same study reports a <strong>97.56% efficacy rate</strong> against <strong>3.95%</strong> in controls, and that number will
be quoted at you. It deserves unpacking, because it is true and misleading at the same time.</p>
<p>"Efficacy" there means <strong>any improvement</strong> on a global aesthetic scale. Inside the treatment group,
the distribution was:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Rating</th>
<th>Share of treated patients</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Improved</td>
<td>50.87%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Much improved</td>
<td>41.11%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Very much improved</td>
<td>5.57%</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>So: almost everyone moved, and <strong>half of them moved by the smallest measurable amount</strong>. "97% of
patients improved" and "most patients improved a little" describe the same data.</p>
<p>That is not a criticism of the treatment — a reliable small improvement with a 1.67% adverse event
rate is a reasonable thing to buy. It is a caution about the number, which is exactly the sort of
figure that ends up on a clinic's English landing page without its distribution.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-course-involves">What a course involves</h2>
<p>Boosters are sold as a course: several sessions at intervals, then maintenance. The injections are
numerous and shallow, so a topical anaesthetic is usual, and the characteristic short-term effect is
small raised bumps at each injection point, with redness and sometimes bruising, settling over days.</p>
<p>Because improvements in the trial were sustained to about three months after the final session, the
maintenance interval is not arbitrary — but it does mean the cost is recurring. Ask for the total of
the proposed course before starting it, not the price per session.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask-a-korean-clinic">Questions to ask a Korean clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li>What exactly is being injected, and what is its Korean product name?</li>
<li>Which MFDS portal is it licensed on — device or drug?</li>
<li>Is it hyaluronic acid, polynucleotide, or a mixture? If a mixture, of what?</li>
<li>How many sessions, at what interval, and what is the total?</li>
<li>What are you expecting to change, and how will we judge it?</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>A skin booster is a technique — shallow injections across an area to change skin quality — not a

three months with adverse events in 1.67%. Its widely quoted 97.56% efficacy rate means almost
