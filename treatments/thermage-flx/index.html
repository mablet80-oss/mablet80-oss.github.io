Thermage and monopolar radiofrequency: what depth buys you

Monopolar radiofrequency heats deep and diffusely to contract collagen. How it differs from the other RF devices Korean clinics sell, and what trials report.

Short answer

What does Thermage do, and how does it differ from the other RF devices?

Thermage is a monopolar radiofrequency device: it passes current through tissue so the tissue's own resistance heats it, contracting collagen immediately and prompting new collagen over the following months. Monopolar is the deepest and most diffuse of the radiofrequency arrangements, reaching up to around 20 mm, and also the most painful. It tightens skin that is in place; it does not reposition tissue that has descended.

Key facts

Monopolar radiofrequency — one active electrode, current returning through a grounding pad
Purpose
Tightening lax skin and softening contour on the face and body
Pain

Sessions
Usually a single treatment, reassessed after some months
Results timeline
An immediate contraction effect, then gradual change as new collagen forms

What monopolar radiofrequency is

Radiofrequency treatment does not heat the skin from outside. It passes an electrical current through tissue, and the tissue's own resistance turns that current into heat from within. Heated collagen contracts immediately, and the controlled injury prompts the body to lay down new collagen over the following months.

Thermage is the monopolar version: one active electrode on the skin, with the current returning through a grounding pad elsewhere on the body. That arrangement is what produces its defining characteristic — energy spread through a large volume of tissue, deep.

The three arrangements, and why the difference matters

A Korean clinic will offer you radiofrequency under half a dozen device names without ever mentioning which arrangement each uses. The arrangement is the useful information.

Monopolar reaches deepest — up to around 20 mm according to the 2025 review cited here — with diffuse energy distribution, and is the most painful. Bipolar confines the energy between two electrodes at 1–4 mm, which is shallower and more controlled. Microneedle radiofrequency puts the energy through needles, so it is delivered past the epidermis to a depth the operator sets.

Mapped onto what you will actually be offered in Seoul: Thermage, Oligio and Volnewmer are monopolar; InMode is bipolar; Potenza and Sylfirm are microneedle. The question "which arrangement is this?" tells you more about what a device will do than its brand name does.

What the evidence reports

The 2025 review in Health Science Reports gathers what has been published. Its headline figures for monopolar devices:

  • A multicentre trial reporting 83.2% periorbital improvement and eyebrow lifting greater than 0.5 mm in 61.5% of 86 patients.
  • Satisfaction across studies ranging from 50% to 82%.
  • 73.5% observing improvement within two months.

That satisfaction range is worth pausing on. A spread from half to four-fifths of patients is not a rounding difference; it reflects genuinely different results across studies, devices, and operators. Any single satisfaction figure quoted to you is a selection from that range.

The reviewers' own conclusion is the sentence to take away: efficacy "depends critically on the customization of treatment parameters and the expertise of the practitioner."

Pain, cooling, and what can go wrong

Depth costs comfort. Monopolar is the most uncomfortable of the three arrangements, precisely because it is heating a large volume.

It also carries the clearest hazard, which is burning. These devices apply surface cooling to protect the epidermis while the deeper tissue heats, and the review notes the trade-off explicitly: more cooling protects the surface but works against the depth you were paying for. Getting that balance right is a setting the operator chooses, which is the practical meaning of "expertise of the practitioner".

What it does not do

  • It does not reposition tissue. Descended tissue is a thread lift or surgical question.
  • It does not add volume. A hollow is a filler question.
  • It is not immediate in its final form. There is a contraction effect on the day, but the substantive change accrues over months, and judging it at two weeks is judging it too early.

Questions to ask a Korean clinic

  • Which device, and is it monopolar, bipolar, or microneedle?
  • What is its Korean product name, so I can check the licence?
  • How many shots and at what energy level, and how is that decided for me?
  • Who operates it — and are they a doctor?
  • If you are also proposing focused ultrasound, what is each one for?

The bottom line

Monopolar radiofrequency heats a large, deep volume of tissue — up to around 20 mm — contracting collagen immediately and prompting more over months. Published satisfaction ranges from 50% to 82%, which is wide, and the reviewers attribute the variation to treatment parameters and operator expertise rather than to the technology alone. It tightens; it does not lift descended tissue or replace lost volume.

Frequently asked questions

Thermage or Ultherapy?
Focused ultrasound is delivered at fixed depths and reaches the SMAS in a targeted way; monopolar radiofrequency heats a large volume of tissue diffusely, up to around 20 mm. Neither repositions tissue. Clinics often propose both, so ask what each is addressing in your case rather than accepting them as a package.
Is Oligio the same as Thermage?
Oligio and Volnewmer are Korean monopolar radiofrequency devices, so they sit in the same category as Thermage and are usually cheaper per session. The published evidence discussed here is for monopolar radiofrequency as an arrangement, and does not establish that one device performs like another. Ask what the claim of equivalence is based on.
How deep does it actually go?
The 2025 review cited below gives monopolar radiofrequency a penetration depth of up to 20 mm, against 1–4 mm for bipolar. That depth is the argument for it, and it is also why it hurts more and why surface cooling is needed to protect the skin.
What does the evidence show?
The review reports a multicentre trial finding 83.2% periorbital improvement and eyebrow lifting greater than 0.5 mm in 61.5% of 86 patients, with satisfaction across studies ranging from 50% to 82% and 73.5% seeing change within two months. Note the width of that satisfaction range — it is doing more work than a single headline number would.
Is it safe?
The main hazard is burning, which is why these devices apply surface cooling, and the review is explicit that outcomes depend on parameter customisation and operator expertise. Who runs the device matters at least as much as which device it is.
What will it not do?
It will not lift tissue that has descended — that is a thread lift or surgery question — and it will not fill a hollow. If the change you want is repositioning rather than tightening, this is the wrong category.
